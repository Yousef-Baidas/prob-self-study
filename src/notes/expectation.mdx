## 4.1 Mean of a random variable

Toss two coins together, sixteen times, and count the heads on each toss. Call that count
`headCount`. Over the sixteen tosses, no heads turns up $4$ times, one head turns up $7$
times, and two heads turns up $5$ times. Averaging the count the ordinary way,

$$
\frac{(0)(4)+(1)(7)+(2)(5)}{16}=1.06.
$$

Regroup the same arithmetic around the *fractions* of tosses landing on each value —
$4/16$, $7/16$, $5/16$ — and the total number of tosses drops out entirely:

$$
(0)\left(\frac{4}{16}\right)+(1)\left(\frac{7}{16}\right)+(2)\left(\frac{5}{16}\right)=1.06.
$$

Those fractions are exactly the long-run relative frequencies for `headCount`, so this
same $1.06$ is what a person tossing two coins over and over would see as the *average*
number of heads per toss — whether the total number of tosses were $16$ or $16{,}000$.
This long-run average is the **mean of the random variable**, also called the
**mathematical expectation** or **expected value**.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'headCount', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'the mean of headCount', symbol: '\\mu_X,\\ \\text{or just}\\ \\mu,\\ \\text{or}\\ E(X)' },
  ]}
/>

An average need not be a value the measurement ever actually takes — $1.06$ heads never
happens on a single toss, just as a salesperson's average monthly income rarely equals any
one month's paycheque. The mean is a summary of the whole distribution, not a prediction
about any one outcome.

**Definition 4.1.** For a discrete measurement, weight every value by its own probability
and add:

$$
\mu=E(X)=\sum_x xf(x).
$$

For a continuous measurement, the sum becomes an integral over the density:

$$
\mu=E(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} xf(x)\,dx.
$$

<InCode
  title="Expectation is a weighted sum, and the weights are the probabilities"
  note="The continuous case is the same reduce with the sum replaced by a limit — an integral against the density instead of a running total against a pmf."
>
```ts
const mean = (values: number[], mass: number[]) =>
  values.reduce((total, x, i) => total + x * mass[i], 0);

// mean([0, 1, 2, 3], [1/35, 12/35, 18/35, 4/35]) === 12 / 7
```
</InCode>

Unlike the *sample mean* of Chapter 1, which averages data you actually collected, $E(X)$
is computed straight from the probability distribution — it describes the population the
data would be drawn from, before any sampling happens.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.1, Example 4.1). A lot of $7$ components holds $4$ good and
> $3$ defective ones. A sample of $3$ is drawn. Call the number of good components in the
> sample `goodCount`, written $X$. Its pmf is hypergeometric,
>
> $$
> f(x)=\frac{\dbinom{4}{x}\dbinom{3}{3-x}}{\dbinom{7}{3}},\qquad x=0,1,2,3,
> $$
>
> giving $f(0)=\tfrac{1}{35}$, $f(1)=\tfrac{12}{35}$, $f(2)=\tfrac{18}{35}$,
> $f(3)=\tfrac{4}{35}$. Then
>
> $$
> \mu=E(X)=(0)\frac{1}{35}+(1)\frac{12}{35}+(2)\frac{18}{35}+(3)\frac{4}{35}=\frac{12}{7}=1.7.
> $$
>
> A sample of $3$, drawn over and over from this lot, contains on average $1.7$ good
> components — a number `goodCount` itself can never equal, since it only takes whole
> values.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.1, Example 4.3). A device's life in hours, `deviceLife`, has
> density $f(x)=20{,}000/x^3$ for $x>100$. Then
>
> $$
> \mu=E(X)=\int_{100}^{\infty} x\cdot\frac{20{,}000}{x^3}\,dx=\int_{100}^{\infty}\frac{20{,}000}{x^2}\,dx=200,
> $$
>
> so this type of device lasts, on average, $200$ hours.

### A function of the measurement

Often the quantity worth averaging is not `headCount` itself but something built from it —
twice `headCount` minus one, or `headCount` squared. Call such a rule `g`, so the new
quantity is $g(X)$. Whenever `headCount` takes the value $2$, $g(X)$ takes the value
$g(2)$: the two are linked value-for-value.

**Theorem 4.1.** The mean of $g(X)$ is found the same way as the mean of $X$ itself, just
weighting $g(x)$ instead of $x$:

$$
\mu_{g(X)}=E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)f(x) \quad\text{(discrete)},
\qquad
\mu_{g(X)}=E[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)f(x)\,dx \quad\text{(continuous)}.
$$

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'a rule built from headCount', symbol: 'g(X)' },
    { name: 'the mean of that rule', symbol: '\\mu_{g(X)}=E[g(X)]' },
  ]}
/>

<InCode
  title="E(g(X)) is the same reduce, with g applied to the values before they are weighted"
  note="Only the values passed in change — the mass array, mass, is untouched, because g never rewrites the probabilities."
>
```ts
const meanOf = (g: (x: number) => number, values: number[], mass: number[]) =>
  values.reduce((total, x, i) => total + g(x) * mass[i], 0);

// meanOf((x) => 2 * x - 1, [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], carCountMass) is E(2X - 1)
```
</InCode>

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.1, Example 4.4). Cars passing a car wash between 4 and 5
> p.m., `carCount` (written $X$), has pmf $f(4)=\tfrac{1}{12}$, $f(5)=\tfrac{1}{12}$,
> $f(6)=\tfrac{1}{4}$, $f(7)=\tfrac{1}{4}$, $f(8)=\tfrac{1}{6}$, $f(9)=\tfrac{1}{6}$. The
> attendant is paid $g(X)=2X-1$ dollars. His expected pay is
>
> $$
> E[2X-1]=\sum_{x=4}^{9}(2x-1)f(x)\approx\$12.67.
> $$

**Do not compute $g(E(X))$ instead of $E[g(X)]$.** For the example above,
$g(E(X))=2E(X)-1$ happens to equal $E[2X-1]$ *only because $g$ is linear* — Theorem 4.5,
next section, is exactly the statement that a linear $g$ commutes with the mean. For a
non-linear $g$, such as $g(X)=X^2$, the two numbers are generally different, and computing
$g(\mu)$ in place of $E[g(X)]$ is one of the most common errors in this chapter.

Extending Theorem 4.1 to two measurements $X$ and $Y$ with a joint distribution:

**Definition 4.2.** The mean of $g(X,Y)$ is

$$
\mu_{g(X,Y)}=E[g(X,Y)]=\sum_x\sum_y g(x,y)f(x,y) \quad\text{(discrete)},
\qquad
\mu_{g(X,Y)}=E[g(X,Y)]=\int\!\!\int g(x,y)f(x,y)\,dx\,dy \quad\text{(continuous)}.
$$

Setting $g(X,Y)=X$ recovers $E(X)$ from the joint table by summing $x$ against the
*marginal* $g(x)$ of §3.4 — the joint table and the marginal agree on $E(X)$, because
collapsing the other variable away never changes a sum already taken over every value of
$x$.

## 4.2 Variance and covariance of random variables

Two companies each send out an average of $2$ cars a day for official business — same
mean, `carCount` for company A and company B alike. But company A's daily count sits
tightly around $2$, while company B's count swings from $0$ to $4$. The mean alone cannot
tell these two situations apart; a second number is needed to describe how *spread out*
the distribution is.

**Definition 4.3.** The **variance** of `carCount`, written $\sigma^2$ or $\text{Var}(X)$,
is the mean squared distance of the measurement from its own mean:

$$
\sigma^2=E[(X-\mu)^2]=\sum_x(x-\mu)^2f(x) \quad\text{(discrete)},
\qquad
\sigma^2=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-\mu)^2f(x)\,dx \quad\text{(continuous)}.
$$

The positive square root, $\sigma$, is the **standard deviation**. Squaring the deviations
before averaging is what keeps a value below the mean from cancelling one above it; it
also means $\sigma^2$ carries the *square* of whatever unit `carCount` is measured in,
while $\sigma$ carries the same unit as `carCount` itself. A variance in litres-squared is
not directly comparable to one in litres, which is exactly why the standard deviation,
not the variance, is the number quoted alongside a mean.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'the variance', symbol: '\\sigma^2,\\ \\text{or}\\ \\text{Var}(X)' },
    { name: 'the standard deviation', symbol: '\\sigma' },
  ]}
/>

**Theorem 4.2** gives a computational shortcut that avoids re-centring every value before
squaring it:

$$
\sigma^2=E(X^2)-\mu^2.
$$

<InCode
  title="Variance by definition and variance by the shortcut are the same function of the data, computed two ways"
  note="Both take (values, mass) and return the same number — the shortcut just avoids building an intermediate array of squared deviations."
>
```ts
const variance = (values: number[], mass: number[]) => {
  const mu = mean(values, mass);
  return meanOf((x) => (x - mu) ** 2, values, mass); // Definition 4.3
};

const varianceShortcut = (values: number[], mass: number[]) =>
  meanOf((x) => x ** 2, values, mass) - mean(values, mass) ** 2; // Theorem 4.2
```
</InCode>

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.2, Example 4.9). The number of defective parts,
> `defectCount`, out of $3$ sampled has $f(0)=0.51$, $f(1)=0.38$, $f(2)=0.10$,
> $f(3)=0.01$. Then $\mu=0.61$ and $E(X^2)=0.87$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma^2=0.87-(0.61)^2=0.4979.
> $$

Variance is never negative — it averages squared distances, and a square cannot be
negative. That single fact is worth holding onto, because covariance, defined next, does
not share it.

**Theorem 4.3** extends Definition 4.3 to a function of the measurement, exactly as
Theorem 4.1 extended the mean: $\sigma^2_{g(X)}=E\{[g(X)-\mu_{g(X)}]^2\}$, computed by
weighting $[g(x)-\mu_{g(X)}]^2$ by $f(x)$.

### Covariance: do two measurements move together?

Draw two refills from a box of pens — `blueCount` and `redCount` from §3.4 — and ask
whether a large `blueCount` tends to come with a large or a small `redCount`.

**Definition 4.4.** The **covariance** of `blueCount` and `redCount`, written $\sigma_{XY}$
or $\text{Cov}(X,Y)$, is

$$
\sigma_{XY}=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=\sum_x\sum_y(x-\mu_X)(y-\mu_Y)f(x,y).
$$

If large `blueCount` tends to pair with large `redCount` (and small with small), the
deviations $(X-\mu_X)$ and $(Y-\mu_Y)$ tend to share a sign, their product tends to be
positive, and the covariance comes out positive. If large tends to pair with small, the
covariance comes out negative. **Covariance can be negative — unlike variance, which never
is.**

**Theorem 4.4** gives the same kind of computational shortcut Theorem 4.2 gave for
variance:

$$
\sigma_{XY}=E(XY)-\mu_X\mu_Y.
$$

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.2, Example 4.13). For the pen table of §3.4,
> $E(XY)=\tfrac{3}{14}$, $\mu_X=\tfrac{3}{4}$, $\mu_Y=\tfrac{1}{2}$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_{XY}=\frac{3}{14}-\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=-\frac{9}{56}.
> $$
>
> Drawing more blue pens leaves fewer red ones to draw, so the two counts move in opposite
> directions — exactly what the negative sign says.

**If `blueCount` and `redCount` are statistically independent, their covariance is $0$**
(Corollary 4.5, next section). **The converse fails**: a zero covariance does *not* prove
independence. Covariance only measures a *linear* tendency to move together; two
measurements can be tangled in a curved, nonlinear way that leaves the covariance at
exactly $0$ while the measurements remain dependent.

### The correlation coefficient

The size of $\sigma_{XY}$ depends on the units `blueCount` and `redCount` happen to be
measured in, so a covariance of $-9/56$ cannot, by itself, say whether the relationship is
*strong*. Rescaling by both standard deviations removes the units.

**Definition 4.5.** The **correlation coefficient** is

$$
\rho_{XY}=\frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}.
$$

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'the covariance', symbol: '\\sigma_{XY},\\ \\text{or}\\ \\text{Cov}(X,Y)' },
    { name: 'the correlation coefficient', symbol: '\\rho_{XY}' },
  ]}
/>

$\rho_{XY}$ is free of whatever units $X$ and $Y$ carry, and it always satisfies
$-1\le\rho_{XY}\le1$: it is $0$ exactly when $\sigma_{XY}=0$, and it reaches $\pm1$ only
when $Y$ is an *exact* linear function of $X$. Because $\rho_{XY}$ is unit-free and bounded,
it — not the raw covariance — is the number to compare across two different pairs of
measurements.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.2, Example 4.15). Continuing the pen example,
> $\sigma_X^2=\tfrac{45}{112}$ and $\sigma_Y^2=\tfrac{9}{28}$, so
>
> $$
> \rho_{XY}=\frac{-9/56}{\sqrt{(45/112)(9/28)}}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\approx-0.447.
> $$
>
> A *larger* covariance in magnitude does not automatically mean a *stronger* relationship
> — Walpole's Example 4.16 has a smaller covariance than this one but a larger $|\rho_{XY}|$,
> which is exactly why the raw covariance is the wrong number to compare.

## 4.3 Means and variances of linear combinations of random variables

If `machineA` and `machineB` each produce some items per day, `machineA + machineB` is the
combined daily output of the plant. This section collects the rules for means and
variances of such combinations — rules that hold for *any* distribution, discrete or
continuous, and need no more than the means, variances and covariances already in hand.

**Theorem 4.5.** For constants $a$ and $b$,

$$
E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b.
$$

Two special cases are worth naming: setting $a=0$ gives $E(b)=b$ (Corollary 4.1 — the mean
of a constant is that constant), and setting $b=0$ gives $E(aX)=aE(X)$ (Corollary 4.2).

**Theorem 4.6.** The mean of a sum or difference of functions of one measurement is the sum
or difference of their means: $E[g(X)\pm h(X)]=E[g(X)]\pm E[h(X)]$.

**Theorem 4.7 and Corollary 4.4.** The same holds across two measurements:
$E[g(X,Y)\pm h(X,Y)]=E[g(X,Y)]\pm E[h(X,Y)]$, and in particular
$E(X\pm Y)=E(X)\pm E(Y)$ — always, whether or not $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

**Theorem 4.8 and Corollary 4.5.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $E(XY)=E(X)E(Y)$, and it
follows that $\sigma_{XY}=0$. (This is the fact used in §4.2 above; independence forces
zero covariance, but — as stressed there — not the other way around.)

### Combining variances

**Theorem 4.9.** For constants $a$, $b$, $c$,

$$
\sigma^2_{aX+bY+c}=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\sigma_{XY}.
$$

<InCode
  title="Var(aX + bY) keeps the cross term unless independence is known to erase it"
  note="Dropping the covariance parameter instead of passing 0 is exactly the forgetting-the-cross-term error the misconceptions list below calls out."
>
```ts
const varOfCombo = (a: number, b: number, varX: number, varY: number, covXY: number) =>
  a ** 2 * varX + b ** 2 * varY + 2 * a * b * covXY; // Theorem 4.9

const varOfComboIndependent = (a: number, b: number, varX: number, varY: number) =>
  varOfCombo(a, b, varX, varY, 0); // Corollary 4.9 — the special case covXY = 0
```
</InCode>

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'the combined measurement', symbol: 'aX+bY+c' },
    { name: 'its variance', symbol: '\\sigma^2_{aX+bY+c}' },
  ]}
/>

> **Worked example** (Walpole §4.3, Example 4.22). $X$ and $Y$ have $\sigma_X^2=2$,
> $\sigma_Y^2=4$, $\sigma_{XY}=-2$. For $Z=3X-4Y+8$,
>
> $$
> \sigma_Z^2=(3)^2(2)+(-4)^2(4)+2(3)(-4)(-2)=18+64+48=130.
> $$

A handful of corollaries fall out of Theorem 4.9 by setting terms to zero:

- **Corollaries 4.6–4.7.** Shifting by a constant never changes variance:
  $\sigma^2_{aX+c}=a^2\sigma_X^2$, and in particular $\sigma^2_{X+c}=\sigma_X^2$. Adding or
  subtracting a constant slides every value the same distance without changing how spread
  out they are.
- **Corollary 4.8.** Scaling by a constant scales variance by its *square*:
  $\sigma^2_{aX}=a^2\sigma_X^2$.
- **Corollaries 4.9–4.11.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, the cross term vanishes
  (Corollary 4.5) and Theorem 4.9 simplifies to
  $\sigma^2_{aX\pm bY}=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2$ — note the **plus** sign even for the
  *difference* $aX-bY$, because the term being removed, $2ab\sigma_{XY}$, was already zero.
  The pattern extends to any number of mutually independent measurements:
  $\sigma^2_{a_1X_1+\cdots+a_nX_n}=a_1^2\sigma_{X_1}^2+\cdots+a_n^2\sigma_{X_n}^2$.

Independence is what removes the covariance term; nothing else in Theorem 4.9 depends on
the distributions of $X$ and $Y$ at all.

## 4.4 Potential misconceptions and hazards

Mistakes this chapter actually punishes, gathered in one place:

- **$E[g(X)]\neq g(E(X))$ in general.** The two agree when $g$ is linear (Theorem 4.5) and
  otherwise generally differ. Plugging $\mu$ into $g$ instead of averaging $g(x)$ over the
  whole distribution is the single most common shortcut error in this chapter.
- **Zero covariance does not prove independence.** Independence forces $\sigma_{XY}=0$
  (Corollary 4.5); the converse is false. Covariance only detects a *linear* tendency to
  move together, so two measurements tangled nonlinearly can show $\sigma_{XY}=0$ while
  remaining thoroughly dependent.
- **Variance is never negative; covariance can be.** $\sigma^2$ averages squared
  deviations, so it is always $\ge0$. $\sigma_{XY}$ averages a *product* of two deviations
  that can carry opposite signs, so it can land anywhere in $[-\sigma_X\sigma_Y,\
  \sigma_X\sigma_Y]$, including negative values.
- **Variance and standard deviation carry different units.** $\sigma^2$ is in the squared
  unit of the measurement; $\sigma$ is in the same unit as the measurement itself. A
  variance is not comparable across two measurements with different units, and neither
  variance nor standard deviation from one kind of measurement (heights) is comparable to
  either from an unrelated kind (aptitude scores) — only distributions sharing a unit are
  worth comparing this way.
- **A mean can be a value the measurement never takes.** `headCount` averaging $1.06$ heads
  per toss, or `goodCount` averaging $12/7$ good components, are both long-run averages —
  neither number is ever the outcome of any single trial.
