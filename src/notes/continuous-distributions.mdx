## 6.1 Continuous uniform distribution

A conference room can be booked for no more than 4 hours, and both short and long
meetings happen often enough that no length is more likely than any other. Call the length
of a booked meeting `meetingLength`. Chapter 3 introduced the density function as the curve
whose *area* gives a probability; this chapter's job is to name the handful of curve shapes
that show up over and over. The flattest possible shape — every length in the interval
equally likely — is the first one.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'the interval endpoints', symbol: 'A,\\ B' },
    { name: 'meetingLength', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'the flat density on [A, B]', symbol: 'f(x; A, B)' },
  ]}
/>

**Continuous uniform distribution.** On the interval `[A, B]`,

$$
f(x; A, B) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{B-A}, & A \le x \le B, \\ 0, & \text{elsewhere.} \end{cases}
$$

The curve is a rectangle of base $B-A$ and constant height $1/(B-A)$ — which is exactly what
makes the area under it equal to $1$, and why this distribution is also called the
**rectangular distribution**. The interval need not be closed; $(A, B)$ works just as well,
since a single point contributes no area.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.1). A conference lasts a `meetingLength` uniform on
> $[0, 4]$ hours. (a) The density is $f(x) = 1/4$ for $0 \le x \le 4$. (b) The chance a
> conference lasts at least 3 hours is $P(X \ge 3) = \int_3^4 \tfrac14\,dx = \tfrac14$.

**Mean and variance.**

$$
\mu = \frac{A+B}{2}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{(B-A)^2}{12}.
$$

Both are exactly what you would guess without any calculus: the mean sits at the interval's
midpoint by symmetry, and the variance grows with the square of the interval's width, the
same way it did for every distribution in Chapter 4.

**Cumulative distribution function.** Integrating the flat density from `A` up to `x` gives

$$
F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < A, \\[4pt] \dfrac{x-A}{B-A}, & A \le x < B, \\[4pt] 1, & x \ge B. \end{cases}
$$

Because the density is constant, $F$ climbs in a straight line from $0$ at $x=A$ to $1$ at
$x=B$ — the CDF of a uniform variable is as flat-out simple as CDFs get, and it hands back
$P(x_1 \le X \le x_2) = F(x_2) - F(x_1) = (x_2-x_1)/(B-A)$ without ever touching the density
directly.

<InCode title="The uniform CDF is a clamped linear ramp">
```ts
const clamp = (value: number, low: number, high: number) =>
  Math.min(Math.max(value, low), high);

const uniformCdf = (x: number, A: number, B: number) =>
  clamp((x - A) / (B - A), 0, 1);
```
</InCode>

## 6.2 Normal distribution

Heights, measurement errors, manufactured part dimensions — an enormous range of physical
measurements cluster symmetrically around a typical value, tapering off the same way on
either side. Call such a measurement `physicalMeasurement`. The bell-shaped curve that
describes it is the single most important continuous shape in statistics: the **normal
distribution**, sometimes called the **Gaussian distribution** after Karl Friedrich Gauss,
who derived its equation studying measurement errors (Abraham DeMoivre found the equation
first, in 1733).

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'physicalMeasurement', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'the mean — where the curve is centered', symbol: '\\mu' },
    { name: 'the standard deviation — how spread out the curve is', symbol: '\\sigma' },
    { name: 'the normal density at x', symbol: 'n(x; \\mu, \\sigma)' },
  ]}
/>

**Normal distribution.**

$$
n(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\, e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(x-\mu)^2}, \qquad -\infty < x < \infty.
$$

Once `mean` and `standard deviation` are fixed, the whole curve is fixed. Sliding `mean`
left or right moves the curve without changing its shape; growing `standard deviation`
flattens and widens the curve, since the total area beneath it always has to stay $1$.
Five properties fall out of inspecting the curve and its derivatives:

1. The peak sits exactly at $x = \mu$.
2. The curve is symmetric about the vertical line through $\mu$.
3. It bends from concave-down to concave-up at $x = \mu \pm \sigma$ — the inflection points.
4. The curve approaches, but never touches, the horizontal axis in either direction.
5. The total area under the curve is $1$.

**Theorem 6.2.** The mean and variance of `physicalMeasurement`, when it follows
$n(x;\mu,\sigma)$, are exactly the parameters that name the curve: mean $\mu$ and variance
$\sigma^2$. The substitution $z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$ inside $E(X-\mu)$ and $E[(X-\mu)^2]$ is
what makes both integrals collapse to $0$ and $\sigma^2$ respectively — the same
change of variable this chapter leans on again in §6.3.

The normal distribution's importance goes well past describing measurements directly: it
also shows up as a *limit*. Under the right conditions it approximates the binomial and
hypergeometric distributions (§6.5 covers the binomial case), and later chapters show that
averages of samples tend toward a normal shape almost regardless of what the underlying
measurement looks like — the foundation for most of the estimation and testing that follows
in this book.

## 6.3 Areas under the normal curve

A density curve is only useful once its area answers a question. For `physicalMeasurement`
between two values `lowerBound` and `upperBound`, the chance of landing in that range is the
shaded area of the curve between them:

$$
P(x_1 < X < x_2) = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} n(x; \mu, \sigma)\,dx.
$$

Nobody wants to integrate this by hand for every pair of $\mu$, $\sigma$, so instead every
normal curve is converted into *one* reference curve first. Rescale `physicalMeasurement` by
how many standard deviations it sits from its own mean, and call that rescaled quantity the
**standard normal variable**, written $Z$:

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'the standard normal variable', symbol: 'Z = \\dfrac{X-\\mu}{\\sigma}' },
    { name: 'the standard normal density (mean 0, variance 1)', symbol: 'n(z; 0, 1)' },
  ]}
/>

<InCode title="Standardizing and unstandardizing are algebraic inverses">
```ts
const standardize = (x: number, mean: number, sd: number) =>
  (x - mean) / sd;

const unstandardize = (z: number, mean: number, sd: number) =>
  z * sd + mean;

// unstandardize(standardize(x, mean, sd), mean, sd) === x
```
</InCode>

**Definition 6.1.** The distribution of a normal random variable with mean $0$ and variance
$1$ is the **standard normal distribution**.

Because $Z$ is a linear rescaling of `physicalMeasurement`, an interval $[x_1, x_2]$ maps to
the interval $[z_1, z_2]$ with $z_i = (x_i - \mu)/\sigma$, and the two areas are identical:

$$
P(x_1 < X < x_2) = P(z_1 < Z < z_2).
$$

<Figure width={CHART_WIDTH.standardization} caption="Standardizing slides and rescales the horizontal axis — the region's boundaries move, but its area does not. Both panels show exactly the same probability.">

This is what makes a single table — the standard normal table, tabulating
$P(Z < z)$ — enough for every normal distribution that ever comes up, instead of needing a
separate table for every possible $(\mu,\sigma)$ pair.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.2). For the standard normal distribution:
> (a) the area to the right of $z=1.84$ is $1 - 0.9671 = 0.0329$;
> (b) the area between $z=-1.97$ and $z=0.86$ is $0.8051 - 0.0244 = 0.7807$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.3). Reading the table in reverse — from a known area
> to a $z$ value: (a) $P(Z > k) = 0.3015$ gives $k = 0.52$; (b) $P(k < Z < -0.18) = 0.4197$
> gives $k = -2.37$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.4). `physicalMeasurement` is normal with $\mu = 50$,
> $\sigma = 10$. The chance it lands between $45$ and $62$: standardize to
> $z_1 = -0.5$, $z_2 = 1.2$, then
>
> $$
> P(45 < X < 62) = P(-0.5 < Z < 1.2) = 0.8849 - 0.3085 = 0.5764.
> $$

Standardization also runs in reverse: given a target area, look up the $z$ that leaves it,
then solve $x = \sigma z + \mu$ for the boundary itself.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.6). For $\mu=40$, $\sigma=6$: the value with $45\%$ of
> the area to its left is $x = (6)(-0.13)+40 = 39.22$; the value with $14\%$ of the area to
> its right is $x = (6)(1.08)+40 = 46.48$.

## 6.4 Applications of the normal distribution

The same three steps — standardize, look up the table, translate back if needed — answer
almost every question in this section.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.7). Battery life is normal, mean $3.0$ years,
> $\sigma = 0.5$ year. The chance a battery lasts less than $2.3$ years: $z = -1.4$, so
> $P(X < 2.3) = P(Z < -1.4) = 0.0808$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.9). A ball bearing's diameter is normal with
> $\mu = 3.0$ cm, $\sigma = 0.005$ cm; the specification is $3.0 \pm 0.01$ cm. Standardizing
> both limits gives $z = \pm 2.0$, so $P(2.99 < X < 3.01) = 0.9772 - 0.0228 = 0.9544$, and the
> expected scrap rate is $1 - 0.9544 = 0.0456$, or $4.56\%$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.13). Exam scores are normal, mean $74$, $\sigma = 7$.
> To find the lowest A, given the top $12\%$ get one: a $z$ leaving $12\%$ to the right leaves
> $88\%$ to the left, which the table gives as $z = 1.18$, so
> $x = (7)(1.18) + 74 = 82.26$ — the lowest A is $83$, the highest B is $82$.

A discrete count measured to the nearest whole unit and then modelled with a normal curve
(Walpole Examples 6.11–6.12, comparing a resistor's resistance measured exactly versus
rounded to the nearest ohm) needs the same half-unit correction this chapter formalises next,
in §6.5 — rounding shifts *which* boundary the area is measured to.

## 6.5 Normal approximation to the binomial

Table lookups for `b(x; n, p)` get unwieldy once `n` is large, and computing a *sum* of many
binomial terms by hand is worse still. Since a binomial histogram with a large `n` and a `p`
not too close to $0$ or $1$ already looks bell-shaped, it is natural to ask whether the
normal curve with the same mean and variance can stand in for it.

**Theorem 6.3.** If `defectiveCount` is binomial with mean $\mu = np$ and variance
$\sigma^2 = npq$, then as $n \to \infty$,

$$
Z = \frac{X - np}{\sqrt{npq}}
$$

converges to the standard normal distribution.

A binomial bar at `x` is an *exact* probability, $b(x;n,p)$; the matching slice of normal
curve area is only ever an approximation to it, and the two are compared by centering the
slice on the bar rather than on a single point. Read a bar at `x` as covering the interval
`[x - 0.5, x + 0.5]` — the **continuity correction** — since a discrete count is being stood
in for by a continuous curve.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'the continuity-corrected boundary', symbol: 'x \\pm 0.5' },
    { name: 'the approximating normal parameters', symbol: '\\mu = np,\\quad \\sigma = \\sqrt{npq}' },
  ]}
/>

**Normal approximation to the binomial.** For large `n`,

$$
P(X \le x) \approx P\!\left(Z \le \frac{x + 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{npq}}\right),
$$

and the approximation is good whenever **both** `np > 5` **and** `n(1-p) > 5` hold — check
both, not just one; a `p` near $0$ or $1$ can satisfy one and badly fail the other.

<InCode title="The continuity correction is a ±0.5 shift applied before standardizing" note="Drop the ±0.5 and z changes -- a different, and wrong, number, not just a less precise one.">
```ts
const continuityCorrectedZ = (
  x: number, mean: number, sd: number, direction: 1 | -1,
) => (x + direction * 0.5 - mean) / sd;

// P(X <= x) includes the whole bar at x, so direction = +1.
// P(X >= x) also includes the whole bar at x, so direction = -1.
```
</InCode>

Standardizing $x_1 = 3.5$ and $x_2 = 4.5$ against $\mu = 6$, $\sigma = 1.897$ gives
$z_1 = -1.32$ and $z_2 = -0.79$, so $b(4;15,0.4) \approx P(-1.32 < Z < -0.79) = 0.2148 -
0.0934 = 0.1214$ — close to the exact $0.1268$ from the table, and the approximation only
improves for sums of several bars at once.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.15). $100$ patients, each recovering with chance
> $0.4$. With $\mu = 40$, $\sigma = 4.899$, the chance fewer than $30$ survive is
> $P(X < 30) \approx P(Z < -2.14) = 0.0162$, using the continuity-corrected boundary $29.5$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole Example 6.16). Guessing on $80$ four-choice questions, $p =
> 1/4$: $\mu = 20$, $\sigma = 3.873$. The chance of $25$ to $30$ correct guesses uses the
> corrected boundaries $24.5$ and $30.5$, giving $z_1 = 1.16$, $z_2 = 2.71$, so
> $P(25 \le X \le 30) \approx P(1.16 < Z < 2.71) = 0.9966 - 0.8770 = 0.1196$.

## 6.6 Exponential distribution

A Poisson process (§5.5) counts events — cars passing an intersection, calls reaching a
switchboard, particles hitting a detector — over a fixed stretch of time. But "how many
events in the next hour" is not the only question that stretch of time supports. Turn the
question around: instead of fixing the time and counting events, fix your attention on *one*
event and ask how long you wait for it. Call that wait `waitTime`.

Suppose cars arrive at an intersection at an average rate of $\lambda = 6$ cars per hour.
Asking *"how many cars arrive in the next 10 minutes?"* fixes the time window and counts a
number that can only be $0, 1, 2, \dots$ — a discrete random variable, so this is a **Poisson**
question. Asking instead *"how long until the next car arrives?"* fixes nothing about time in
advance; the answer might be $0.5$ minutes or $2.37$ minutes or $4.128$ minutes — a continuous
random variable, so this is an **exponential** question. Same process, same rate `λ`, two
different random variables depending on which quantity is held fixed and which is measured.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'rate of events per unit time', symbol: '\\lambda' },
    { name: 'waitTime — time to the next event', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'the exponential density at x', symbol: 'f(x; \\lambda)' },
  ]}
/>

**Exponential distribution.** The waiting time to the first event of a Poisson process with
rate `λ` is an **exponential random variable** with parameter `λ`:

$$
f(x; \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x \ge 0.
$$

**Where the formula comes from.** `waitTime` exceeds some value `x` exactly when *zero*
events land in the interval $[0, x]$ — a Poisson question with mean $\lambda x$, answered by
the Poisson formula at $0$: $p(0; \lambda x) = e^{-\lambda x}$. So the **survival function**
of `waitTime` is

$$
P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x},
$$

and its cumulative distribution function is the complement,

$$
F(x) = P(X \le x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x \ge 0.
$$

Differentiating $F$ recovers the density above. Every exponential fact this section uses
traces back to this one survival function, so it is worth keeping in view rather than
memorising the density in isolation.

**Mean and variance.**

$$
\mu = E(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \sigma^2 = V(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}.
$$

Since $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} = 1/\lambda$ too, **the exponential distribution's mean and
standard deviation are the same number**, $1/\lambda$. This is the flip side of a fact from
Chapter 5: **the Poisson distribution's mean and variance are the same number**, $\lambda t$.
The two contrasts are easy to swap under exam pressure — Poisson matches mean to *variance*,
exponential matches mean to *standard deviation* — so it is worth saying explicitly rather
than trusting memory for which one is which.

> **Worked example** (corporate log-ons). User log-ons to a large corporate network form a
> Poisson process averaging $\lambda = 25$ log-ons per hour. Let `waitTime`, in hours, be the
> time from the start of an interval until the first log-on.
>
> (a) The chance there are no log-ons in the next $6$ minutes ($0.1$ hour):
>
> $$
> P(X > 0.1) = e^{-25(0.1)} = e^{-2.5} \approx 0.082.
> $$
>
> (b) The chance the wait is between $2$ and $3$ minutes ($0.033$ and $0.05$ hour):
>
> $$
> P(0.033 < X < 0.05) = F(0.05) - F(0.033) = e^{-25(0.033)} - e^{-25(0.05)} \approx 0.152.
> $$
>
> (c) The interval of time such that the chance *no* log-on occurs during it is $0.90$: solve
> $e^{-25x} = 0.90$ for `x`,
>
> $$
> -25x = \ln(0.90) \implies x = \frac{-\ln(0.90)}{25} \approx 0.0042 \text{ hour} \approx 0.25 \text{ minute}.
> $$
>
> (d) The mean and standard deviation of the wait until the next log-on are both
> $1/\lambda = 1/25 = 0.04$ hour $= 2.4$ minutes — the mean-equals-standard-deviation fact
> above, not a coincidence of this example.

**The lack-of-memory property.** For an exponential `waitTime`, the chance of waiting `t₂`
*more* time units, given that `t₁` time units have already passed with nothing happening, is
exactly the same as the chance of waiting `t₂` time units from scratch:

$$
P(X < t_1 + t_2 \mid X > t_1) = P(X < t_2).
$$

This is not an extra assumption bolted onto the exponential — it falls straight out of the
survival function derived above. Writing the conditional probability out and using
$P(X>t_1)=e^{-\lambda t_1}$:

$$
P(X < t_1+t_2 \mid X > t_1) = 1 - P(X > t_1 + t_2 \mid X > t_1)
= 1 - \frac{P(X > t_1+t_2)}{P(X > t_1)}
= 1 - \frac{e^{-\lambda(t_1+t_2)}}{e^{-\lambda t_1}} = 1 - e^{-\lambda t_2} = P(X < t_2).
$$

The $e^{-\lambda t_1}$ in the numerator and denominator cancel exactly, leaving no trace of
`t₁` behind. That cancellation is the whole property: the tail of the curve beyond `t₁`,
rescaled by dividing out $P(X>t_1)$, is not merely *similar in shape* to the original curve —
it is the *same curve*, restarted at zero.

<InCode title="Lack of memory is an assertion you can run, not just derive" note="t1 = 1.5 never appears on the right -- it cancelled algebraically above before this code ever ran.">
```ts
const survive = (t: number, rate: number) => Math.exp(-rate * t);

const lhs = survive(1.5 + 1, 0.4) / survive(1.5, 0.4);
const rhs = survive(1, 0.4);

// lhs and rhs agree to floating-point precision.
```
</InCode>

Dividing the left panel by S(t₁) reproduces the right panel exactly — the tail is a rescaled copy of the whole curve, not just a shape that resembles it.">

> **Worked example** (Geiger counter). Let `waitTime` be the time between particle detections
> on a Geiger counter, exponential with $E(X) = 1.4$ minutes, so $\lambda = 1/1.4$. The chance
> a particle is detected in the next $30$ seconds ($0.5$ minute):
>
> $$
> P(X < 0.5) = F(0.5) = 1 - e^{-0.5/1.4} \approx 0.300.
> $$
>
> Now suppose $3$ minutes have passed with *no* detection. Does the wait become more likely to
> end soon because a detection is somehow "due"? Compute the conditional probability directly:
>
> $$
> P(X < 3.5 \mid X > 3) = \frac{F(3.5) - F(3)}{1 - F(3)} = \frac{0.035}{0.117} \approx 0.30.
> $$
>
> Exactly the same $0.30$ as the unconditional answer above. **No** — a quiet spell carries no
> information about how soon the next detection arrives; the counter has no memory of the last
> $3$ minutes at all. This is the intuition behind the name: a component or process with this
> property neither wears out nor "warms up" with age, so betting that an overdue event is
> close is a bet the mathematics does not support.

## 6.7 Potential misconceptions and hazards

Mistakes this chapter actually punishes, gathered in one place:

- **Assuming normality without checking it.** The single biggest misuse of this chapter's
  material is treating a measurement as normal purely because normal curves are convenient
  to work with. Later chapters supply goodness-of-fit tests and graphical checks
  specifically because this assumption is made constantly and is not automatically true.
- **Skipping the continuity correction.** Approximating a discrete count with a continuous
  curve without shifting the boundary by $\pm 0.5$ systematically mismatches the bar being
  approximated with the wrong slice of curve — the error is small for a single bar but
  compounds across a sum of many. Confirm `np > 5` **and** `n(1-p) > 5` before trusting the
  approximation at all.
- **Standardizing is a change of variable, not a new formula.** $Z = (X-\mu)/\sigma$ turns
  *this* normal curve into *the* standard one so a single table suffices; skipping the
  standardization step and reading $x$ off the standard-normal table directly reads the
  wrong number entirely.
- **Table A.3 is a left-tail table.** It tabulates $P(Z < z)$ — the area to the *left* of
  `z` — not the area to the right. Reading a table value as $P(Z > z)$ without taking the
  complement $1 - P(Z<z)$ silently flips every right-tail answer to its complement.
- **Using `β` where the problem gives `λ`.** This course's exponential is parameterised by
  the rate `λ` (events per unit time), with $f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, $\mu = 1/\lambda$,
  and $\sigma^2 = 1/\lambda^2$ — not by Walpole's mean `β`, where $f(x) = (1/\beta)e^{-x/\beta}$
  and $\mu = \beta$. The two are reciprocals of each other: plugging a rate directly into a
  mean-parameterised formula (or vice versa) gives the reciprocal of the correct answer, not
  a number that is merely off.
- **Assuming the exponential's mean is also its median.** The exponential is right-skewed, so
  the two are not the same value. Solving $F(x) = 0.5$ gives the median
  $x = \ln(2)/\lambda \approx 0.693/\lambda$, strictly *less* than the mean $1/\lambda$ — more
  than half of the probability sits below the mean, not evenly split around it the way it
  would be for a symmetric normal curve.
- **The lack-of-memory property is a property, not a default.** It holds for the exponential
  distribution and nothing else in this chapter. A component that genuinely wears with age
  does not stop wearing just because the exponential is convenient to compute with — check
  that memorylessness is a reasonable assumption for the process at hand before reaching for
  this distribution, rather than after.
- **Every parameter in this chapter's examples was treated as known.** In practice, `μ`,
  `σ`, and `λ` all have to be estimated from data, and an estimate carries its own
  uncertainty that a plugged-in "known" parameter does not. Chapters 9 onward take up
  estimation directly; this chapter's exact answers all rest on the parameters being exactly
  what the problem states them to be.

## 6.8 Calculator shortcuts on the fx-991CW

Every normal-distribution question in this chapter ends at the same wall: you need an area
under a curve that has no closed-form antiderivative. Table A.3 is one way through it. The
calculator's Distribution app is the other, and it is faster, needs no interpolation between
rows, and cannot be misread off the line above the one you wanted. Learn the handful of
question shapes below and the table becomes a way to check your answer rather than the way
to get it.

Press `HOME`, select the **Distribution** icon, press `OK`. Four of the seven calculation
types on that menu carry this whole chapter: `Normal CD` for an area, `Inverse Normal` for a
cut-off, `Binomial CD` for the exact answer §6.5 is approximating, and `Poisson PD` for a
cross-check on §6.6.

<Notation title="The calculator calls this" rows={[
  { name: 'the left edge of the region you want', symbol: '\\texttt{Lower}' },
  { name: 'the right edge of the region you want', symbol: '\\texttt{Upper}' },
  { name: 'the area to the left of an unknown cut-off', symbol: '\\texttt{Area}', note: 'Inverse Normal takes this and hands back the cut-off itself.' },
  { name: 'the cut-off it hands back', symbol: 'x\\text{Inv}' },
]} />

You type the real `μ` and `σ` from the problem, not $0$ and $1$. The app standardizes
internally, so §6.3's change of variable becomes a step you no longer perform by hand — which
is exactly why you must still understand it, since the exam can ask for the `z` value on its
own.

Two behaviours surprise people mid-question, and both are worth knowing before the clock
starts:

- **Normal PD, Normal CD and Inverse Normal never show the List/Variable menu.** They take a
  single value at a time, and the result is stored in `Ans` so you can chain from it.
- **Inverse Normal's tail is always the left tail.** There is no setting to flip it. An
  upper-tail percentage must be converted to `1 - Area` before you type anything.

### The normal question shapes

This is the part to memorize. The left column is how the question is phrased; the right column
is what you enter. Nothing else varies.

| What the question asks | App | What to enter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $P(a < X < b)$ — "between $a$ and $b$" | Normal CD | `Lower` $= a$, `Upper` $= b$ |
| $P(X > a)$ — "at least", "exceeds", "more than" | Normal CD | `Lower` $= a$, `Upper` $= 1 \times 10^{99}$ |
| $P(X < b)$ — "at most", "under", "less than" | Normal CD | `Lower` $= -1 \times 10^{99}$, `Upper` $= b$ |
| "Find the value below which 15% fall" | Inverse Normal | `Area` $= 0.15$ |
| "Find the value above which 15% fall" | Inverse Normal | `Area` $= 0.85$ |
| "Find the $z$ value for a 5% tail" | Inverse Normal | `Area` $= 0.05$, with $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$ |

The $10^{99}$ in rows two and three is the calculator's stand-in for infinity, typed with the
`×10ˣ` key. Any value ten or more standard deviations past the mean gives the same answer to
displayed precision, so the exact magnitude does not matter.

The two reverse problems — a stated tail percentage that fixes an unknown `μ` or an unknown
`σ` — are the same Inverse Normal call followed by one line of algebra. Read `z` off the
standard curve, then rearrange $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$:

$$
\mu = x - z\sigma \qquad\qquad \sigma = \frac{x - \mu}{z}
$$

A lower-tail percentage under $50\%$ gives a negative `z`, which is what pushes the mean
*above* the limit. Losing that sign is the standard wrong answer here, and the calculator will
not catch it for you.

### The normal approximation, and its own answer key

Section 6.5 approximates a binomial count with a normal curve. The calculator will compute
both sides of that approximation, which turns the exercise into a self-checking one:

1. Compute the approximation the way the chapter asks. Set $\mu = np$ and
   $\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}$, then use Normal CD with the continuity correction —
   `Lower` $= x_1 - 0.5$ and `Upper` $= x_2 + 0.5$.
2. Then run `Binomial CD` with the original `N` and `p`. That is the exact probability, with
   no approximation anywhere in it.

The two should agree to roughly three decimals whenever $np > 5$ and $n(1-p) > 5$. A gap much
wider than that almost always means the $\pm 0.5$ was dropped. Write down the normal
approximation as your answer, since that is what the question asked for, but use the exact
value to catch yourself.

### The exponential has no app

There is no exponential distribution in the Distribution menu. This chapter's most heavily
examined section is the one the calculator helps with least, so the formulas have to be in
your head and typed with the `e^` key:

| What the question asks | What to compute |
| --- | --- |
| "survives past $x$", "lasts longer than $x$" | $e^{-\lambda x}$ |
| "fails before $x$" | $1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ |
| "given it has already lasted $t_1$, survives another $t_2$" | $e^{-\lambda t_2}$ — discard $t_1$ |
| mean time, variance | $1/\lambda$ and $1/\lambda^2$ |
| median time | $\ln(2)/\lambda$, not $1/\lambda$ |
| a series system of components | add the rates, then use $\lambda_{\text{total}}$ |

There is one genuine cross-check available. Waiting past time $x$ means the Poisson count over
that stretch is zero, so `Poisson PD` with $x = 0$ and $\lambda = \lambda x$ returns exactly
$e^{-\lambda x}$. If you are ever unsure whether you have inverted the rate, run it both ways
and keep the one that matches — this is the fastest way to catch the `β`-for-`λ` error
described in §6.7.

The continuous uniform of §6.1 needs no shortcut at all. Its probabilities, mean and variance
are arithmetic you can do faster than you can open a menu.

### Habits worth drilling

- **Store `μ` and `σ` into variables `A` and `B`** with the `STO` key before a multi-part
  question. Every later part then reuses them instead of re-typing them.
- **Do not leave the app between parts.** The parameter screen keeps its last values, so a
  four-part question about one normal curve means editing `Lower` and `Upper` and pressing
  `EXE` again.
- **Show the `z` anyway.** A rubric that asks for the standardized value wants to see it,
  even when the app never made you compute it. Getting it from Inverse Normal with $\mu = 0$
  and $\sigma = 1$ costs a few seconds.
- **The app cannot tell you which distribution to use.** Deciding between a normal, a
  binomial, and an exponential from the wording of a problem is the part of the exam that no
  shortcut touches — and it is where the marks actually are.
