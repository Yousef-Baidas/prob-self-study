## Descriptive statistics

**Descriptive statistics** summarise a sample with single numbers that capture its
centre, its spread, and the general shape of the data — as opposed to **inferential**
statistics, which uses the sample to draw conclusions about the wider population.

### Measures of location

- **Sample mean** $\bar{x}$ — the arithmetic average; the *centroid* (balance point) of the data.
- **Sample median** $\tilde{x}$ — the middle value of the sorted data; unaffected by extreme values.

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i,
\qquad
\tilde{x} =
\begin{cases}
x_{(n+1)/2} & n \text{ odd} \\[4pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(x_{n/2} + x_{n/2+1}\right) & n \text{ even}
\end{cases}
$$

Here $x_1,\dots,x_n$ are the observations ($x_i$ the $i$-th one), sorted in increasing
order for the median, and $n$ is the sample size.

> **Mean vs. median.** For the data $2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 100$ the mean is
> $\bar{x} = \tfrac{117}{5} = 23.4$, but the median is $\tilde{x} = 5$. The single large
> value drags the mean far from the bulk of the data, while the median still reflects
> the centre — the median **resists outliers**.

### Measures of variability

- **Sample range** — the crudest measure of spread, $R = x_{\max} - x_{\min}$.
- **Sample variance** $s^2$ and **sample standard deviation** $s$ — the standard measures.

$$
s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2,
\qquad
s = \sqrt{s^2}
$$

The divisor $n-1$ is the **degrees of freedom**: because the deviations always satisfy
$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$, only $n-1$ of them are free to vary. Variance is in
*squared* units; the standard deviation is back in the original units of the data.

> **Worked example — pH‑meter readings** (Walpole §1.4, Example 1.4). Ten readings:
>
> $$
> 7.07,\ 7.00,\ 7.10,\ 6.97,\ 7.00,\ 7.03,\ 7.01,\ 7.01,\ 6.98,\ 7.08.
> $$
>
> The mean is $\bar{x} = \dfrac{70.25}{10} = 7.025$; the variance is
> $s^2 = \dfrac{1}{9}\big[(7.07-7.025)^2 + \dots + (7.08-7.025)^2\big] = 0.001939$; and the
> standard deviation is $s = \sqrt{0.001939} = 0.044$, on $n-1 = 9$ degrees of freedom.

## Types of data

Data are described along two independent axes.

| Axis | Kinds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nature** | **categorical** (qualitative) — sorts items into classes | pass / fail, colour |
| | **numerical** (quantitative) — counts or measurements | number of defects, weight |
| **Values** | **discrete** — countable, isolated values | arrivals per day |
| | **continuous** — any value in an interval | yield in grams |

For **binary** (two‑category) data, if $x$ of $n$ units fall in the first category the
**sample proportion** is

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}.
$$

Because each unit is coded $1$ or $0$, this proportion is itself just a sample mean of
ones and zeros.

> **Worked example.** In a trial of $n = 50$ patients, $x = 20$ improve. The sample
> proportion that improved is $\hat{p} = \tfrac{20}{50} = 0.4$.

## Populations and samples

- **Population** — the collection of *all* individuals or items of a particular type
  (every board the firm will ever make, every possible measurement of a process).
- **Sample** — the subset of observations actually collected from the population.
- **Statistical inference** — using the sample to draw conclusions about the population.
  The reasoning runs from the specific to the general, so it is **inductive** and carries
  uncertainty; the elements of probability let us quantify that uncertainty.
- **Parameter vs. statistic** — a fixed number describing the *population* (e.g. the
  population mean) is a **parameter**; a number computed from the *sample* (e.g. $\bar{x}$)
  is a **statistic**, used as an **estimate** of the parameter.
- **Simple random sample** — one chosen so that every sample of the same size is equally
  likely to be selected. A sample that systematically misses part of the population is a
  **biased sample**.

> **Why probability comes first.** Suppose a process is acceptable when it produces no
> more than 5% defectives. If a random sample of 100 items contains 10 or more defectives —
> an outcome whose probability under an acceptable process is only about $0.028$ — that is
> strong evidence the process is *not* acceptable. Probability turns sample data into an
> inferential conclusion (Walpole §1.1, Example 1.1).
