## 1.1 Overview: statistical inference, populations, and probability

You inspect a batch of 100 manufactured items and count how many are defective. Call
that count `defectCount`. That single number is the starting point for everything in
this chapter: **descriptive statistics** summarise a sample like this one with single
numbers that capture its centre, its spread, and its general shape — as opposed to
**inferential** statistics, which uses the sample to draw a conclusion about the wider
population it came from.

- **Population** — the collection of *all* individuals or items of a particular type
  (every board the firm will ever make, every possible measurement of a process).
- **Sample** — the subset of observations actually collected from the population. Your
  100 inspected items are a sample; the population is every item the process will ever
  produce.
- **Statistical inference** — using the sample to draw a conclusion about the
  population. The reasoning runs from the specific (this sample's `defectCount`) to the
  general (is the whole process acceptable?), so it is **inductive** and carries
  uncertainty; the elements of probability let us quantify that uncertainty.
- **Parameter vs. statistic** — a fixed number describing the *population* is a
  **parameter**. A number computed from the *sample* is a **statistic**, used as an
  **estimate** of the parameter.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'a number computed from the sample', symbol: '\\bar{x}', note: 'a statistic — used to estimate the matching population parameter' },
]} />

> **Why probability comes first.** Suppose a process is acceptable when it produces no
> more than 5% defectives. If a random sample of 100 items contains 10 or more
> defectives — an outcome whose probability under an acceptable process is only about
> $0.028$ — that is strong evidence the process is *not* acceptable. Probability turns
> `defectCount` into an inferential conclusion (Walpole §1.1, Example 1.1).

That $0.028$ has a name. It is the probability, *assuming the process really is
acceptable*, of seeing a sample at least this extreme — 10 or more defectives out of
100. A result this unlikely under the assumption is what makes the assumption worth
doubting.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'the probability of a result this extreme, if the assumption being tested is true', symbol: 'P\\text{-value}', note: '0.028 here — small enough to doubt the process is acceptable' },
]} />

## 1.2 Sampling procedures and experimental design

Say you want to know the average income across a state. You cannot ask everyone, so
you pick a sample instead — and *how* you pick it decides whether your conclusion
means anything.

- **Simple random sample** — one chosen so that every sample of the same size is
  equally likely to be selected. A sample that systematically misses part of the
  population is a **biased sample** — e.g. surveying only urban households when the
  population is an entire state, or only the households that happen to answer the
  phone.
- **Stratified random sampling** — when the population splits naturally into
  non-overlapping, homogeneous groups (*strata*), drawing a separate random sample from
  *within* each stratum guarantees that no group is over- or under-represented. This is
  a refinement of simple random sampling, not an alternative to randomness.

### Designed experiments

Take a comparison of coated versus uncoated aluminium specimens, tested for how many
stress cycles each survives before failing.

- **Treatments** (or **factor combinations**) — the conditions being compared or
  studied (coated vs. uncoated, or drug vs. placebo).
- **Experimental unit** — the specimen, patient, or item a treatment is applied to; it
  is the source of the variability *within* a treatment group. If that within-group
  variability is too large, it can "wash out" a real difference between treatments.
- **Completely randomized design** — experimental units are assigned to treatments *at
  random*. Randomization is what lets the analyst attribute a difference in outcomes to
  the treatment rather than to some uncontrolled, confounding difference between the
  units (age, batch, operator, weight, …).

Section 1.7 below classifies designed experiments against two other kinds of study
where the analyst does **not** get to assign treatment levels.

## 1.3 Measures of location: the sample mean and median

Ten deliveries arrive with these delays, in minutes. Which single number best
represents "how late deliveries typically run" — and does that number survive one
unusually bad delivery? Call one delay `deliveryDelay`.

- **Sample mean** — the arithmetic average of `deliveryDelay` across every delivery;
  the *centroid* (balance point) of the data.
- **Sample median** — the middle `deliveryDelay` value once the deliveries are sorted
  from shortest to longest delay; unaffected by extreme values.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'the delivery delays', symbol: 'x_1,\\dots,x_n', note: 'n deliveries, in the order collected' },
  { name: 'the average delay', symbol: '\\bar{x}', note: 'the sample mean' },
  { name: 'the middle delay', symbol: '\\tilde{x}', note: 'the sample median' },
]} />

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i,
\qquad
\tilde{x} =
\begin{cases}
x_{(n+1)/2} & n \text{ odd} \\[4pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(x_{n/2} + x_{n/2+1}\right) & n \text{ even}
\end{cases}
$$

Here $x_1,\dots,x_n$ are the observations ($x_i$ the $i$-th one), sorted in increasing
order for the median, and $n$ is the sample size.

> **Mean vs. median.** Five deliveries have these delays, in minutes: $2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\
> 100$. The mean is $\bar{x} = \tfrac{117}{5} = 23.4$, but the median is
> $\tilde{x} = 5$. The single large value drags the mean far from the bulk of the data,
> while the median still reflects the centre — the median **resists outliers**.

### Trimmed mean

The **$p\%$ trimmed mean** sorts the deliveries by delay, discards the largest $p\%$
and the smallest $p\%$ of them, and averages what remains. It is a compromise between
the mean (uses every value, so it is easily swayed by outliers) and the median (uses
only the middle one or two values, so it ignores most of the data). The sample median
is, in fact, the extreme case of a trimmed mean in which everything but the middle
observation(s) is trimmed away.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'the trimmed average', symbol: '\\bar{x}_{\\mathrm{tr}(p)}' },
]} />

> **Worked example.** Ten delivery delays, in minutes, sorted: $2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7,\ 8,\
> 9,\ 10,\ 11,\ 50$.
>
> The mean is $\bar{x} = \tfrac{111}{10} = 11.1$, and the median is
> $\tilde{x} = \tfrac{1}{2}(7+8) = 7.5$. The $10\%$ trimmed mean removes the largest
> $10\%$ ($1$ value: $50$) and the smallest $10\%$ ($1$ value: $2$), leaving $8$ values:
>
> $$
> \bar{x}_{\mathrm{tr}(10)} = \frac{3+5+6+7+8+9+10+11}{8} = \frac{59}{8} = 7.375.
> $$
>
> The trimmed mean lands close to the median and far from the mean — the single
> outlier delay of $50$ minutes pulled $\bar{x}$ well above where the bulk of the
> deliveries actually sit, while trimming it away (along with the smallest value, to
> keep the trim symmetric) recovers a number much more typical of the sample.

A **dot plot** is the simplest way to see this directly: each delivery delay is drawn
as a single dot along a number line, so a value that does not belong with the rest —
like the 50-minute delivery below — stands out on sight, before any arithmetic.

<Figure width={CHART_WIDTH.dotPlot} caption="A single 50-minute delivery pulls the mean well past the bulk of the data; the median barely moves.">

## 1.4 Measures of variability

You calibrate a pH meter and take ten readings of the same buffer solution. Call one
reading `phReading`. The readings won't all agree — how spread out are they around
their average?

- **Sample range** — the crudest measure of spread, $R = x_{\max} - x_{\min}$.
- **Sample variance** and **sample standard deviation** — the standard measures of
  spread around the mean.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'how far the readings reach', symbol: 'R = x_{\\max}-x_{\\min}', note: 'the sample range' },
  { name: 'typical squared distance from the average reading', symbol: 's^2', note: 'the sample variance' },
  { name: "typical distance from the average, in the reading's own units", symbol: 's = \\sqrt{s^2}', note: 'the sample standard deviation' },
]} />

$$
s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2,
\qquad
s = \sqrt{s^2}
$$

The divisor $n-1$ is the **degrees of freedom**: because the deviations always satisfy
$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$, only $n-1$ of them are free to vary. Variance is in
*squared* units; the standard deviation is back in the original units of the data. (The
sample variance is used more in inferential *theory*; the standard deviation, sharing
the data's own units, is used more in applications.)

> **Worked example — pH‑meter readings** (Walpole §1.4, Example 1.4). Ten readings:
>
> $$
> 7.07,\ 7.00,\ 7.10,\ 6.97,\ 7.00,\ 7.03,\ 7.01,\ 7.01,\ 6.98,\ 7.08.
> $$
>
> The mean is $\bar{x} = \dfrac{70.25}{10} = 7.025$; the variance is
> $s^2 = \dfrac{1}{9}\big[(7.07-7.025)^2 + \dots + (7.08-7.025)^2\big] = 0.001939$; and
> the standard deviation is $s = \sqrt{0.001939} = 0.044$, on $n-1 = 9$ degrees of
> freedom.

### A computational shortcut for $s^2$

Computing $s^2$ from its definition means subtracting the mean from every reading,
squaring each difference, and adding those up — every deviation gets computed twice
if you also want $\bar{x}$ checked. A short algebraic rearrangement needs only the
readings themselves and their squares, and returns exactly the same number.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'the readings, added up', symbol: '\\textstyle\\sum x_i' },
  { name: 'the readings, squared and then added up', symbol: '\\textstyle\\sum x_i^2' },
]} />

$$
s^2 = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 - \dfrac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\right)^2}{n}}{n-1}.
$$

> **Check against the pH-meter readings above.** $\sum x_i = 70.25$ and
> $\sum x_i^2 = 493.5237$, so
> $s^2 = \dfrac{493.5237 - 70.25^2/10}{9} = \dfrac{0.01745}{9} = 0.001939$ — the same
> variance found the definitional way. Because a small rounding error inside the
> subtraction gets divided by nothing to shrink it back down, this shortcut form is
> more sensitive to rounding readings too early — keep extra decimal places until the
> final division.

### Quartiles and the interquartile range

A queue's wait time, in minutes, splits the sorted data into four equal-sized groups.
How wide is the middle half of the wait times — the part that ignores the fastest and
slowest quarters? Call one measurement `waitTime`.

The **quartiles** cut the sorted `waitTime` values into four parts of roughly equal
size: about a quarter of the observations lie below the first quartile, about half
below the second (which is just the median), and about three quarters below the third.
The **interquartile range** measures spread as the width of that middle half.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'the three cut points', symbol: 'Q_1,\\ Q_2,\\ Q_3', note: 'the first, second (= the median), and third quartiles' },
  { name: 'the width of the middle half', symbol: '\\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1' },
]} />

$$
\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1.
$$

Unlike the range, the IQR ignores both tails, so no single extreme value can inflate
it — it **resists outliers** for the same reason the median does.

Where exactly does a quartile fall in the sorted list? Sort the data first, then
locate $Q_k$ by its **position**:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: "a quartile's position in the sorted list", symbol: 'L_k = \\dfrac{k(n+1)}{4}', note: 'k = 1, 2, 3' },
]} />

$$
L_k = \frac{k(n+1)}{4}, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3.
$$

$L_k$ is a position, not a value. If it is a whole number $j$, the quartile is that
observation, $Q_k = x_j$. If it is fractional, write $L_k = j + f$ with $j$ whole and
$0 < f < 1$; the quartile then sits a fraction $f$ of the way from $x_j$ to the next
observation:

$$
Q_k = x_j + f\left(x_{j+1} - x_j\right).
$$

So a position of $2.75$ means *three quarters of the way from the 2nd observation to
the 3rd*, and you report that exact point — **not** whichever observation happens to
be nearest.

> **Worked example.** Ten wait times, in minutes, already sorted:
>
> $$
> 1,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7,\ 9,\ 12,\ 14,\ 15,\ 18,\ 22 \qquad (n = 10)
> $$
>
> | | position $L_k$ | falls between | quartile |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $Q_1$ | $\tfrac{11}{4} = 2.75$ | $x_2 = 5,\ x_3 = 6$ | $5 + 0.75(6-5) = 5.75$ |
> | $Q_2$ | $\tfrac{22}{4} = 5.5$ | $x_5 = 9,\ x_6 = 12$ | $9 + 0.5(12-9) = 10.5$ |
> | $Q_3$ | $\tfrac{33}{4} = 8.25$ | $x_8 = 15,\ x_9 = 18$ | $15 + 0.25(18-15) = 15.75$ |
>
> The middle half of the wait times is therefore $10$ minutes wide:
> $\mathrm{IQR} = 15.75 - 5.75 = 10$.
>
> **Check $Q_2$ against the median.** With $n = 10$ even, the median formula above
> gives $\tilde{x} = \tfrac{1}{2}(x_5 + x_6) = \tfrac{1}{2}(9 + 12) = 10.5$ — the same
> number. $Q_2$ and $\tilde{x}$ always agree, so computing both is a free check on your
> arithmetic.

> **Careful — other methods give other answers.** Splitting the data at the median and
> taking the median of each half (the rule taught in school), or a calculator's
> default percentile function, would report $Q_1 = 6$ for the data above instead of
> $5.75$. Use the position-and-interpolate rule here.

### Percentiles

The quartiles are one special case of a more general cut. A **percentile** splits the
sorted data so that a stated percentage of the observations lie below it — the
25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles are exactly $Q_1$, $Q_2$, and $Q_3$. The 95th
percentile of `waitTime`, for instance, separates the slowest 5% of queue waits from
the rest. Percentiles use the same position-and-interpolate rule as the quartiles,
just written for an arbitrary cut point instead of quarters:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: "a percentile's position in the sorted list", symbol: 'L_p = \\dfrac{p(n+1)}{100}', note: 'p = the percentile, e.g. 95' },
]} />

$$
L_p = \frac{p(n+1)}{100}.
$$

## 1.5 Discrete and continuous data

Data are described along two independent axes.

| Axis | Kinds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nature** | **categorical** (qualitative) — sorts items into classes | pass / fail, colour |
| | **numerical** (quantitative) — counts or measurements | number of defects, weight |
| **Values** | **discrete** — countable, isolated values | arrivals per day |
| | **continuous** — any value in an interval | yield in grams |

Discrete data are often **count data**: the number of radioactive particles passing a
counter in one millisecond, or the number of oil tankers arriving at a port each day.
Continuous data come from measuring on a continuum — a yield in percent or grams/pound
is free to fall anywhere in an interval.

### Binary data and the sample proportion

You run a clinical trial of 50 patients and count how many improve. Call that count
`improvedCount`. What fraction of patients improved?

For **binary** (two‑category) data like this, if `improvedCount` out of the total
number of patients falls in the first category, the **sample proportion** is the
fraction that does.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'how many improved', symbol: 'x', note: 'the count in the first category' },
  { name: 'how many patients total', symbol: 'n' },
  { name: 'the fraction that improved', symbol: '\\hat{p} = x/n', note: 'the sample proportion' },
]} />

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}.
$$

Because each patient is coded $1$ or $0$ (improved or not), this proportion is itself
just a sample mean of ones and zeros.

> **Worked example.** In a trial of $n = 50$ patients, $x = 20$ improve. The sample
> proportion that improved is $\hat{p} = \tfrac{20}{50} = 0.4$.

## 1.6 Statistical modeling and graphical diagnostics

The end result of a statistical analysis is usually the estimation of parameters of a
**postulated model** — an assumption about the form of the population the data come
from (e.g. that stem weights from Example 1.2 are normally distributed). Before
committing to a model, it pays to *look at the data*: a **scatter plot** of one
variable against another can reveal whether a simple model is even plausible, and can
expose violations of assumptions that a purely numerical summary would miss.
Regression models built on this idea are developed in later chapters; the tools below
are the everyday graphical building blocks used throughout the rest of the book.

An order-processing centre records how many minutes each of twelve orders takes to
process. Call one of those measurements `processingTime`. The same twelve
`processingTime` values, sorted, run through the next three subsections:

$$
23,\ 27,\ 29,\ 31,\ 33,\ 36,\ 38,\ 41,\ 44,\ 45,\ 47,\ 52 \qquad (n = 12).
$$

### Stem-and-leaf display

Split each `processingTime` value into a **stem** (leading digit(s)) and a **leaf**
(the remaining digit), then list the leaves for each stem in increasing order. Rotated
$90°$ counter-clockwise, the columns of leaves form the same silhouette as a
histogram — a stem-and-leaf display is a histogram that keeps every original value.

| Stem | Leaves | Frequency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 3 7 9 | 3 |
| 3 | 1 3 6 8 | 4 |
| 4 | 1 4 5 7 | 4 |
| 5 | 2 | 1 |

With only a few stems, a plot can look too coarse; splitting each stem in two (leaves
0–4 vs. 5–9) doubles the number of classes and is standard practice for larger
samples, such as the book's 40-battery car-life data set (Walpole §1.6, Table 1.5 vs.
Table 1.6).

### Relative frequency distributions and histograms

How much of the sample falls in each range of processing times? Grouping the leaves
above into class intervals of width $10$ and dividing each class's count by $n$ gives
the **relative frequency distribution**:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'how many orders land in a class', symbol: 'f', note: 'the class frequency' },
  { name: 'that count as a share of the whole sample', symbol: 'f/n', note: 'the relative frequency' },
]} />

| Class interval | Midpoint | Frequency $f$ | Relative frequency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20–29 | 25 | 3 | $3/12 \approx 0.250$ |
| 30–39 | 35 | 4 | $4/12 \approx 0.333$ |
| 40–49 | 45 | 4 | $4/12 \approx 0.333$ |
| 50–59 | 55 | 1 | $1/12 \approx 0.083$ |

The relative frequencies sum to $1$ (up to rounding). Plotting a bar of height equal
to the relative frequency over each class interval gives the **relative frequency
histogram** — the picture most people mean by "histogram." As the sample size grows
and the class width shrinks, the jagged outline of a histogram tends toward the smooth
bell-shaped curve used to picture a continuous population **distribution** in later
chapters.

### Symmetry and skewness

A distribution is **symmetric** if it can be folded along a vertical axis so the two
sides coincide. Otherwise it is **skewed**:

- **skewed right** — a long tail toward large values (the histogram's right-hand tail
  is drawn out).
- **skewed left** — a long tail toward small values.

The processing-time histogram above is mildly **right-skewed**: the top class
$(50\text{–}59)$ holds only $1$ observation compared with $3$–$4$ per class lower
down, stretching the tail upward. This matches the arithmetic: the mean
$\bar{x} = 446/12 \approx 37.17$ sits slightly *above* the median $\tilde{x} = 37$ — a
large value in the tail nudges the mean up without moving the median, exactly as in
§1.3's mean-vs-median discussion.

### Box-and-whisker plots and outliers

A **box-and-whisker plot** (box plot) draws a box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ (so its width is
the IQR) with a line at the median, and "whiskers" reaching out to the most extreme
values that are not flagged as **outliers**. How far is too far, before a value counts
as unusual? A common outlier rule flags any observation farther than
$1.5 \times \mathrm{IQR}$ from the box:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'too far below the box', symbol: 'x < Q_1 - 1.5\\,\\mathrm{IQR}' },
  { name: 'too far above the box', symbol: 'x > Q_3 + 1.5\\,\\mathrm{IQR}' },
]} />

$$
\text{outlier if } x < Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR} \quad \text{or} \quad x > Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}.
$$

> **Worked example — continuing the processing-time data** $(n = 12)$. Using
> $L_k = k(n+1)/4$ from §1.4:
>
> $$
> Q_1 = 29 + 0.25(31-29) = 29.5, \qquad
> Q_2 = 36 + 0.5(38-36) = 37, \qquad
> Q_3 = 44 + 0.75(45-44) = 44.75.
> $$
>
> So $\mathrm{IQR} = 44.75 - 29.5 = 15.25$ and $1.5\,\mathrm{IQR} = 22.875$, giving
> fences at $Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR} = 6.625$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR} = 67.625$.
> Since the minimum ($23$) and maximum ($52$) both fall inside the fences, there are
> **no outliers**, and the whiskers reach all the way to $23$ and $52$. A box whose
> median line sits closer to $Q_1$ than to $Q_3$ — as it does here, $37 - 29.5 = 7.5$
> versus $44.75 - 37 = 7.75$ — is a visual flag for the same mild right skew found
> above.

## 1.7 Types of statistical studies: designed experiment, observational study, retrospective study

An engineer tests coated and uncoated aluminium specimens at two humidity levels and
records how many stress cycles each survives before failing. Call that count
`cyclesToFailure`. How the specimens were assigned to those conditions decides what
kind of study this is.

- **Designed experiment** — the analyst chooses the treatments (factor levels) and
  assigns them to experimental units at random (§1.2). Randomization equalizes
  uncontrolled ("nuisance") factors across the treatment groups, so a difference found
  between groups can be attributed to the treatment itself. This gives the strongest
  basis for a causal conclusion.
- **Observational study** — the factor levels are simply recorded, not assigned;
  subjects are monitored as they naturally occur (e.g. tracking blood cholesterol
  against existing blood-sodium levels, since sodium intake cannot ethically be
  assigned). Without randomization, differences found may be caused by some other,
  uncontrolled factor — a **confounding** variable — rather than the factor under
  study.
- **Retrospective study** — uses purely **historical** data, collected over some past
  period for another purpose. It is cheap, since no new data collection is needed, but
  it carries real risks: the validity and reliability of old records is often in
  doubt, values may be missing, collection errors may be undetectable, and the range
  of factor values reflects whatever happened to occur historically rather than a
  range the analyst would have chosen.

| Study type | Who sets the factor levels? | Strength | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Designed experiment | Analyst, assigned at random | Strong causal claims | Requires control over conditions |
| Observational study | Nature / circumstance | Usable when control is impossible | Confounding factors go uncontrolled |
| Retrospective study | Already fixed (historical) | Low cost, no new data needed | Data quality, missingness, irrelevant ranges |

> **Worked example — corrosion coating** (Walpole §1.7, Example 1.3). An engineer
> tests four treatment combinations (coated/uncoated $\times$ 20%/80% humidity) by
> randomly assigning two aluminum specimens to each combination — a completely
> randomized **designed experiment**. Because the assignment is random, an observed
> difference in `cyclesToFailure` across combinations can be attributed to the coating
> and humidity themselves, not to some uncontrolled difference between the specimens.
