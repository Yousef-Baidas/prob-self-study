## 3.1 Concept of a random variable

Test three electronic components as they come off a line, and mark each one `N` for
non-defective or `D` for defective. One outcome of that test is a string such as `NND` —
not a number. A **statistical experiment** is any process like this one, whose repeated
observations are subject to chance; its sample space (Chapter 2) describes outcomes in
words. What we usually want next is a *number* attached to each outcome.

Call the count of defectives `defectiveCount`. It is a **function**: for every outcome in
the sample space it returns one real number. `defectiveCount(NNN) = 0`,
`defectiveCount(NND) = 1`, and so on for every string in

$$
S = \{NNN,\; NND,\; NDN,\; DNN,\; NDD,\; DND,\; DDN,\; DDD\}.
$$

`defectiveCount` is not an event and it is not a probability. It is only the rule that
turns an outcome into a number. Each value `defectiveCount` can take names an **event** —
the set of outcomes sent to it:

$$
\{X = 2\} = \{NDD,\; DND,\; DDN\}.
$$

That is exactly why probabilities can be attached to values of `defectiveCount` at all.
Every statement about `defectiveCount` is shorthand for a statement about a subset of the
sample space.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'defectiveCount', symbol: 'X', note: 'a random variable — write it with a capital letter' },
    { name: 'one value it takes', symbol: 'x', note: 'the matching lowercase letter' },
    { name: 'the event "defectiveCount is 2"', symbol: '\\{X = 2\\} = \\{NDD,\\; DND,\\; DDN\\}' },
  ]}
/>

Random variables split into two kinds, by what their values look like:

- **Discrete measurement** — the sample space holds a finite number of possibilities, or an
  unending sequence with as many elements as there are whole numbers (countably infinite).
  The book calls this a **discrete random variable**. Discrete measurements carry **count
  data**: defectives in a batch, highway fatalities per year, tosses until the first head.
- **Continuous measurement** — the sample space holds an interval of real numbers, so its
  possibilities cannot be listed one by one. The book calls this a **continuous random
  variable**. Continuous measurements carry **measurement data**: heights, weights,
  temperatures, distances, lifetimes.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.1, Example 3.1). Two balls are drawn in succession
> *without replacement* from an urn holding $4$ red and $3$ black balls. Call the count of
> red balls drawn `redCount`. The outcomes and their `redCount` values (written $y$ below)
> are
>
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|c}
> \text{outcome} & y \\ \hline
> RR & 2 \\
> RB & 1 \\
> BR & 1 \\
> BB & 0
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> `redCount` is discrete: it takes only the values $0$, $1$, $2$. The count of possible
> values, not the count of outcomes, is what makes a measurement discrete.

## 3.2 Discrete probability distributions

A shipment of $20$ laptops contains $3$ defective ones. A school buys $2$ at random. Call
the number of defectives among those two `defectivesBought` — it lands on $0$, $1$, or $2$.
Each of those three values happens with some probability. Collecting those probabilities
into a rule, one probability for every possible value, gives a **probability mass
function**, or **pmf**.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'defectivesBought', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'one value it takes', symbol: 'x' },
    { name: 'the pmf — probability of that value', symbol: 'f(x) = P(X = x)' },
  ]}
/>

- A **pmf** is also called the probability function or the probability distribution. The
  set of ordered pairs $(x, f(x))$ is a pmf of a discrete measurement $X$ when, for every
  possible outcome $x$,

$$
f(x) \ge 0,
\qquad \sum_{x} f(x) = 1,
\qquad P(X = x) = f(x).
$$

  These three conditions are a checklist, not a formality: a candidate formula is not a
  distribution until it passes all three. Solving for an unknown constant $c$ is exactly
  the act of forcing $\sum_x f(x) = 1$.

- A pmf can be presented three ways — as a **table** of $x$ against $f(x)$, as a
  **formula** in $x$, or as a **graph** (a bar chart or probability histogram, where the
  area of each bar is the probability of that value). All three carry the same
  information.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.2, Example 3.8). Counting as in §2.3, the favourable
> selections over the $\binom{20}{2} = 190$ equally likely ones give
>
> $$
> f(x) = \frac{\dbinom{3}{x}\dbinom{17}{2-x}}{\dbinom{20}{2}},
> \qquad x = 0, 1, 2,
> $$
>
> so $f(0) = \tfrac{136}{190}$, $f(1) = \tfrac{51}{190}$, $f(2) = \tfrac{3}{190}$. These sum
> to $\tfrac{190}{190} = 1$, as any pmf must.

### The cumulative distribution function

Often the question is not "what is the probability `defectivesBought` equals $x$" but
"what is the probability `defectivesBought` comes in at or below $x$". That accumulated
probability is the **cumulative distribution function**, or **CDF**:

$$
F(x) = P(X \le x) = \sum_{t \le x} f(t), \qquad -\infty < x < \infty.
$$

Three properties are worth committing to memory:

- The CDF is **monotone non-decreasing** — accumulating probability can never lose ground.
- The CDF is defined **for every real number**, not only for the values $X$ actually takes.
  For a discrete measurement it is a **step function**: flat between the values, jumping at
  each one.
- The **jump height at $x$ is $f(x)$**, so the pmf is recoverable from the CDF:
  $f(x) = F(x) - F(x^{-})$, the value just before the jump. For integer-valued $X$ that is
  simply $f(x) = F(x) - F(x-1)$.

Between two values, $P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.2, Examples 3.9–3.10). A car agency sells $50\%$ of its
> inventory with side airbags. Of the next $4$ cars sold, call the number so equipped
> `airbagCount` (written $X$ below), with $f(x) = \dbinom{4}{x}\big/16$ for
> $x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$. Then $f(0) = \tfrac{1}{16}$, $f(1) = \tfrac{4}{16}$,
> $f(2) = \tfrac{6}{16}$, $f(3) = \tfrac{4}{16}$, $f(4) = \tfrac{1}{16}$, and accumulating
> gives
>
> $$
> F(x) = \begin{cases}
> 0, & x < 0, \\
> \tfrac{1}{16}, & 0 \le x < 1, \\
> \tfrac{5}{16}, & 1 \le x < 2, \\
> \tfrac{11}{16}, & 2 \le x < 3, \\
> \tfrac{15}{16}, & 3 \le x < 4, \\
> 1, & x \ge 4.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Reading the pmf back out of it: $f(2) = F(2) - F(1) = \tfrac{11}{16} - \tfrac{5}{16} = \tfrac{6}{16} = \tfrac{3}{8}$.

## 3.3 Continuous probability distributions

### Will the battery last past 3 years?

You measure how long a laptop battery lasts, in years. Call that measurement
`batteryLife`. It can land on any value in a range, so it is a *continuous* measurement.

"How often does the battery die before 3 years?" is an area under a curve. The curve is
tall where lifetimes are common, low where they are rare. Take the area from 0 to 3.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'batteryLife', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'the curve', symbol: 'f(x)', note: 'tall where lifetimes are common — not itself a probability' },
    { name: 'the area from 0 to 3', symbol: 'P(X \\le 3) = \\int_0^3 f(x)\\,dx' },
  ]}
/>

Push that idea one step further. `batteryLife` has probability $0$ of taking **exactly**
any one value — say exactly $3.0000\ldots$ years. Between any two lifetimes, however close
— $2.999$ and $3.001$ years, or even closer — lie infinitely many others, and no single one
of them can carry positive weight without the total exceeding $1$. Only a *range* of
lifetimes has an area, and only area is a probability.

That has a useful consequence: whether an endpoint is included stops mattering.

$$
P(a < X \le b) = P(a < X < b) + P(X = b) = P(a < X < b).
$$

For a continuous measurement, $<$ and $\le$ are interchangeable. (For a discrete
measurement they are emphatically not — see §3.2.)

- **Probability density function (pdf)** — the curve `f(x)` is a pdf for a continuous
  measurement $X$, defined over the reals, when

$$
f(x) \ge 0 \;\text{ for all } x \in \mathbb{R},
\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, dx = 1,
\qquad P(a < X < b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx.
$$

  Read it as **area under the curve**: the probability of an interval is the area over that
  interval, and the total area is $1$. The curve $f(x)$ is *not* itself a probability — it
  may rise above $1$. Only the area is constrained.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.3, Example 3.11). The error in the reaction temperature,
> in $^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, for a controlled experiment is a continuous measurement with
> curve
>
> $$
> f(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^2}{3}, & -1 < x < 2, \\[4pt] 0, & \text{elsewhere.} \end{cases}
> $$
>
> It is a density: $f(x) \ge 0$ throughout, and
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^{2} \frac{x^2}{3}\, dx = \frac{x^3}{9}\bigg|_{-1}^{2} = \frac{8}{9} + \frac{1}{9} = 1.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> P(0 < X \le 1) = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^2}{3}\, dx = \frac{x^3}{9}\bigg|_{0}^{1} = \frac{1}{9}.
> $$

### The cumulative distribution function

The continuous CDF replaces the sum of §3.2 with an integral:

$$
F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\, dt, \qquad -\infty < x < \infty.
$$

Two consequences follow immediately, and between them they cover most exercises:

$$
P(a < X < b) = F(b) - F(a),
\qquad f(x) = \frac{dF(x)}{dx} \;\text{ where the derivative exists.}
$$

So the pdf and the CDF are two views of one object — differentiate to go one way, integrate
to go the other. Where the discrete CDF jumped, this one rises continuously.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.3, Example 3.12). For the density above, integrating from
> the left end gives, for $-1 < x < 2$,
>
> $$
> F(x) = \int_{-1}^{x} \frac{t^2}{3}\, dt = \frac{t^3}{9}\bigg|_{-1}^{x} = \frac{x^3 + 1}{9},
> $$
>
> hence
>
> $$
> F(x) = \begin{cases}
> 0, & x < -1, \\[2pt]
> \dfrac{x^3 + 1}{9}, & -1 \le x < 2, \\[4pt]
> 1, & x \ge 2.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Recovering the earlier answer without re-integrating:
> $P(0 < X \le 1) = F(1) - F(0) = \tfrac{2}{9} - \tfrac{1}{9} = \tfrac{1}{9}$. ✓

## 3.4 Joint probability distributions

Everything so far lives in a **one-dimensional** sample space: one experiment, one number
recorded. Often two numbers come out of the same experiment — the precipitate and the gas
volume from one reaction, the hardness and tensile strength of one copper sample. The
outcome is then a **pair** of numbers, and the sample space is two-dimensional.

Two ballpoint pens are drawn from a box of $3$ blue, $2$ red, and $3$ green. Call the count
of blue pens drawn `blueCount` and the count of red pens drawn `redCount`. Both come out of
the same draw, so their probabilities have to be tracked together, as a **joint
distribution**.

<Notation
  rows={[
    { name: 'blueCount', symbol: 'X' },
    { name: 'redCount', symbol: 'Y' },
    { name: 'the joint pmf', symbol: 'f(x, y) = P(X = x,\\, Y = y)' },
  ]}
/>

- **Joint probability distribution** — for discrete `blueCount` and `redCount`, the
  function $f(x, y)$ is a joint pmf when

$$
f(x, y) \ge 0 \;\text{ for all } (x, y),
\qquad \sum_{x} \sum_{y} f(x, y) = 1,
\qquad P(X = x,\, Y = y) = f(x, y).
$$

  These are the §3.2 conditions with one more index. For any region $A$ of the plane,
  $P[(X,Y) \in A] = \sum_{A} f(x, y)$ — probability over a region is a sum of the cells
  inside it.

- **Joint density function** — for two continuous measurements, the conditions become

$$
f(x, y) \ge 0,
\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y)\, dx\, dy = 1,
\qquad P[(X,Y) \in A] = \iint_{A} f(x, y)\, dx\, dy.
$$

  Where the one-variable case measured area under a curve, this one measures **volume under
  a surface**. Total volume $1$; the probability of a region is the volume standing over it.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.4, Example 3.14). With $\binom{8}{2} = 28$ equally likely
> selections,
>
> $$
> f(x, y) = \frac{\dbinom{3}{x}\dbinom{2}{y}\dbinom{3}{2-x-y}}{\dbinom{8}{2}},
> \qquad 0 \le x + y \le 2,
> $$
>
> which tabulates as
>
> $$
> \begin{array}{c|ccc|c}
> f(x,y) & x=0 & x=1 & x=2 & h(y) \\ \hline
> y=0 & \tfrac{3}{28} & \tfrac{9}{28} & \tfrac{3}{28} & \tfrac{15}{28} \\[2pt]
> y=1 & \tfrac{6}{28} & \tfrac{6}{28} & 0 & \tfrac{12}{28} \\[2pt]
> y=2 & \tfrac{1}{28} & 0 & 0 & \tfrac{1}{28} \\ \hline
> g(x) & \tfrac{10}{28} & \tfrac{15}{28} & \tfrac{3}{28} & 1
> \end{array}
> $$
>
> The nine cells sum to $1$. For the region $A = \{(x,y) \mid x + y \le 1\}$,
>
> $$
> P[(X,Y) \in A] = f(0,0) + f(0,1) + f(1,0) = \frac{3 + 6 + 9}{28} = \frac{9}{14}.
> $$

### Marginal distributions

Sum the joint distribution over the *other* variable and what is left is the distribution of
one variable on its own:

$$
g(x) = \sum_{y} f(x, y),
\qquad h(y) = \sum_{x} f(x, y),
$$

with integrals in place of sums in the continuous case. The name is literal: in the grid
above, $g(x)$ is the column of totals along the bottom and $h(y)$ the row of totals along
the side — the numbers written in the **margins** of the table. Each is a genuine pmf in its
own right, satisfying every condition of §3.2, which is why $P(a < X < b)$ can be computed
from $g$ alone, without ever looking at $Y$.

### Conditional distributions

Conditional probability from §2.6, $P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)$, applied to the events
"`blueCount` equals $x$" and "`redCount` equals $y$", gives a **conditional distribution**:

$$
f(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{g(x)} \;\text{ provided } g(x) > 0,
\qquad
f(x \mid y) = \frac{f(x, y)}{h(y)} \;\text{ provided } h(y) > 0.
$$

The joint distribution goes on top; the marginal of the variable being conditioned **on**
goes underneath. Read it as: pick one row (or column) of the grid above, and rescale its
numbers so they sum to $1$ again. Fixing $x$ and letting $y$ vary, $f(y \mid x)$ is itself a
probability distribution — it is non-negative and sums (or integrates) to $1$, which is
exactly why $P(a < X < b \mid Y = y) = \sum_{a < x < b} f(x \mid y)$ is legitimate.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.4, Example 3.18). From the pen table, conditioning on
> $Y = 1$ uses the row total $h(1) = \tfrac{6}{28} + \tfrac{6}{28} + 0 = \tfrac{3}{7}$, so
>
> $$
> f(x \mid 1) = \frac{f(x, 1)}{h(1)} = \tfrac{7}{3} f(x, 1),
> \qquad
> f(0 \mid 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \quad f(1 \mid 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \quad f(2 \mid 1) = 0.
> $$
>
> So $P(X = 0 \mid Y = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$: given that one of the two pens is red, the other
> is equally likely to be green or blue.

### Statistical independence

If the conditional distribution $f(y \mid x)$ does not depend on $x$ at all, then knowing
`blueCount` tells you nothing about `redCount`. Formally:

$$
X \text{ and } Y \text{ are statistically independent} \iff f(x, y) = g(x)\,h(y)
\;\text{ for all } (x, y) \text{ in range.}
$$

For $n$ variables the same statement becomes
$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = f_1(x_1) \cdots f_n(x_n)$, and they are called **mutually**
statistically independent.

The asymmetry of the test is worth dwelling on. To **disprove** independence, one point
suffices — find any $(x,y)$ with $f(x,y) \neq g(x)h(y)$ and you are done. To **establish**
it, every point must be checked, because a table can agree at some cells and disagree at
others.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §3.4, Example 3.21). Are `blueCount` and `redCount`
> independent? Take the point $(0, 1)$:
>
> $$
> g(0)\,h(1) = \frac{10}{28} \cdot \frac{12}{28} = \frac{5}{14} \cdot \frac{3}{7} = \frac{15}{98},
> \qquad
> f(0, 1) = \frac{6}{28} = \frac{3}{14} = \frac{21}{98}.
> $$
>
> $\tfrac{15}{98} \neq \tfrac{21}{98}$, so `blueCount` and `redCount` are **not**
> independent — and no further cells need checking. (Intuitively: drawing a blue pen uses
> up one of only two slots, so it makes a red draw less likely. The variables are competing
> for the same two draws.)

For continuous measurements the check is usually easier, because a density that **factors**
into a function of $x$ times a function of $y$ over a **rectangular** support is
independent. The rectangle matters: $f(x,y) = 10xy^2$ on $0 < x < y < 1$ factors, but its
support is triangular — the range of $X$ depends on $y$ — and those variables are *not*
independent.

## 3.5 Potential misconceptions and hazards

Mistakes this chapter actually punishes, gathered in one place:

- **A random variable is a function, not an event and not a probability.** `X` is the rule
  mapping outcomes to numbers; "`X` equals 2" is an event; the probability of that event is
  a number. Sliding between the three is the root of most confused notation in this
  chapter.
- **The curve is a probability only in the discrete case.** For a discrete measurement,
  $f(x)$ *is* the probability of $x$. For a continuous measurement, $f$ is a **density**,
  and $f(x) > 1$ is perfectly legal — the density $f(x) = 2x$ on $(0,1)$ reaches $2$. Only
  the total area, $\int f = 1$, is required. Reading a density's height off a graph and
  calling it a probability is the classic error.
- **Zero probability does not mean impossible.** For a continuous measurement every single
  value has probability $0$, yet the experiment must produce *some* value. Probability $0$
  and impossibility part ways as soon as the sample space stops being countable.
- **Endpoints matter for discrete measurements and not for continuous ones.** $P(X \le k)$
  and $P(X < k)$ differ by exactly $f(k)$ when $X$ is discrete — the single commonest
  off-by-one in CDF work — and are equal when $X$ is continuous. Decide which kind of
  measurement you have *before* you decide whether the endpoint is in.
- **The CDF is defined for every real number.** $F(1.5)$ is a perfectly good question about
  an integer-valued measurement; the answer is $F(1)$, because $F$ is flat between the
  jumps. Treating $F$ as defined only at the values the measurement actually takes loses the
  step-function picture and with it the "jump height is $f(x)$" shortcut.
- **Check both conditions before calling something a distribution.** Non-negativity alone
  is not enough, and neither is summing (or integrating) to $1$ alone. When a question hands
  you an unknown constant $c$, that constant exists precisely so you can enforce
  $\sum_x f(x) = 1$ or $\int f = 1$ — solving for it *is* the verification.
- **The two conditional distributions are different things.** $f(x \mid y)$ and
  $f(y \mid x)$ share a numerator, $f(x,y)$, but divide by different marginals. Deciding
  which variable is *given* fixes which total goes underneath; getting it backwards is the
  §3.4 counterpart of confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$ in Chapter 2.
- **The marginals do not determine the joint distribution.** Two different grids can have
  identical row and column totals and describe quite different relationships between the two
  measurements. Marginals throw information away — going from $f(x,y)$ to $g(x)$ and $h(y)$
  is a one-way trip unless the variables happen to be independent.
- **Independence is an all-points claim.** One point with $f(x,y) \neq g(x)h(y)$ disproves
  it outright; one point where they *agree* proves nothing whatsoever. The evidence is
  asymmetric, so a single lucky cell is never a proof of independence.
- **A factoring density is not enough — the support must be rectangular too.** If the range
  of one measurement depends on the value of the other, as with $0 < x < y < 1$, the
  measurements are dependent no matter how cleanly $f(x,y)$ splits into $x$-parts and
  $y$-parts.
