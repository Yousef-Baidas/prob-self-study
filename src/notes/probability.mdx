## 2.1 Sample space

Toss a coin once. Before you toss it, you can write down every result that could happen:
heads, or tails — nothing else is possible. That full list of possible results is the
**sample space** for the experiment.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'the outcome list (one coin toss)', symbol: 'S = \\{H, T\\}' },
  { name: 'the outcome list (one die roll)', symbol: 'S = \\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\\}' },
]} />

Two rules govern how you write a sample space down: the order you list outcomes in carries
no meaning, and no outcome appears twice.

When one outcome has several parts, list it as an **ordered pair**. Roll two dice, and each
outcome is "what the first die showed" paired with "what the second die showed" — 36
outcomes in all, from $(1,1)$ up to $(6,6)$. The order *within* an outcome matters even
though the order you list outcomes in doesn't: rolling a 2 then a 5 is a different outcome
from rolling a 5 then a 2.

<Figure caption="36 equally likely outcomes. (2, 5) and (5, 2) are different cells — which die showed which number matters, even though the order you list outcomes in doesn't.">
  <DiceGrid
    title="6 by 6 grid of two-dice outcomes, with (2,5) and (5,2) ringed as distinct outcomes"
    emphasize={[[2, 5], [5, 2]]}
  />
</Figure>

When listing every outcome is impractical, give the rule they satisfy instead:
$S = \{x \mid x \text{ is a city with over a million people}\}$, read "the set of all $x$
such that …". A sample space is **discrete** when its outcomes are finite or countably
infinite, and **continuous** when it contains an interval of real numbers.

**Tree diagram.** When an experiment runs in stages, a tree lists sample points
systematically: branch once per stage, and each root-to-tip path spells out one sample
point. Flip a coin, and flip again only if it lands heads — otherwise roll a die once —
gives $S = \{HH, HT, T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6\}$, one outcome per path.

## 2.2 Events

You care whether a die roll comes up even, and whether it comes up 4 or higher. Call the
first `evenRoll`, the second `highRoll`. Any such subset of the sample space is called an
**event**.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'evenRoll', symbol: 'A = \\{2, 4, 6\\}' },
  { name: 'highRoll', symbol: 'B = \\{4, 5, 6\\}' },
  { name: 'everything not evenRoll', symbol: "A'", note: 'the complement' },
  { name: 'evenRoll and highRoll together', symbol: 'A \\cap B', note: 'the intersection — outcomes in both' },
  { name: 'evenRoll or highRoll, or both', symbol: 'A \\cup B', note: 'the union — outcomes in either' },
]} />

Three ways to combine events show up constantly, now that each has a name to go with its
symbol:

- **The complement** — everything in $S$ that isn't in evenRoll — is $A' = \{1, 3, 5\}$.
- **The intersection** — everything in both evenRoll and highRoll — is $A \cap B = \{4, 6\}$.
- **The union** — everything in evenRoll or highRoll, or both — is $A \cup B = \{2, 4, 5, 6\}$.

<Figure caption="The union: every outcome that's even, at least 4, or both.">
  <Venn labelA="evenRoll" labelB="highRoll" shade="union" title="Venn diagram of evenRoll union highRoll, both circles shaded" />
</Figure>

<Figure caption="The intersection: only the outcomes the two events share.">
  <Venn labelA="evenRoll" labelB="highRoll" shade="intersection" title="Venn diagram of evenRoll intersect highRoll, only the overlap shaded" />
</Figure>

<Figure caption="The complement of evenRoll: everything left once evenRoll is ruled out.">
  <Venn labelA="evenRoll" labelB="highRoll" shade="complementA" title="Venn diagram of the complement of evenRoll, everything outside the evenRoll circle shaded" />
</Figure>

Two events are **mutually exclusive** (disjoint) when they share no outcomes at all,
$A \cap B = \varnothing$ — so at most one of them can happen on a given trial. `evenRoll`
and "the roll is odd" are mutually exclusive: a single roll can't be both.

Event combinations follow ordinary set algebra: unions and intersections commute and
distribute over each other, and **De Morgan's laws** turn a complement of a union or
intersection into the complement of the pieces:

$$
(A \cup B)' = A' \cap B', \qquad (A \cap B)' = A' \cup B'.
$$

These are the go-to move whenever a question is phrased as a complement of a compound
event — "neither evenRoll nor highRoll" is $(A \cup B)' = A' \cap B'$.

## 2.3 Counting sample points

Suppose a club has 10 members and needs to pick a 3-person committee. How many different
committees are possible? Questions like this — every outcome equally likely, nothing left
to do but count them — need a counting tool, not a probability formula. Four tools cover
almost every case.

**Multiplication rule** — a sequence of $k$ operations, performable in $n_1, n_2, \dots,
n_k$ ways respectively, can be done together in $n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$ ways.

**Permutations** count *ordered* arrangements, where swapping two chosen items gives a
different outcome. Arranging all $n$ objects can be done in $n!$ ways. Arranging $r$ of
them, order mattering, is called `arrangementsOf(n, r)`:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'arrangementsOf(n, r)', symbol: '{}_{n}P_{r} = P(n, r) = \\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}', note: 'ordered — swapping two chosen items counts as a different arrangement' },
  { name: 'n factorial', symbol: 'n! = n(n-1)\\cdots(2)(1), \\quad 0! = 1' },
]} />

The two-argument $P(n, r)$ is a count of arrangements, not a probability — don't confuse
this use of the letter $P$ with the probability $P(A)$ from §2.4.

**Non-distinct objects.** Arranging $n$ items where $n_i$ of them are identical copies of
kind $i$ gives fewer distinct arrangements, since swapping two identical items changes
nothing:

$$
\frac{n!}{n_1!\, n_2! \cdots n_k!}.
$$

**Combinations** count *unordered* selections of $r$ from $n$ — the committee question,
where who's picked matters but not the order they were picked in. Call it
`choicesOf(n, r)`, written three equivalent ways and read "$n$ choose $r$":

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'choicesOf(n, r)', symbol: '{}_{n}C_{r} = C(n, r) = \\binom{n}{r} = \\dfrac{n!}{r!\\,(n-r)!}', note: 'unordered' },
]} />

Here $n$ is the number of objects available and $r$ the number arranged or chosen.

> **Worked examples.**
> - The committee of 3 from 10 people, order irrelevant:
>   $\;{}_{10}C_{3} = \binom{10}{3} = \dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!} = 120$ ways.
> - Distinct arrangements of the letters in **STATISTICS** (S, T each ×3, I ×2, A, C ×1):
>   $\;\dfrac{10!}{3!\,3!\,2!\,1!\,1!} = 50{,}400$.

## 2.4 Probability of an event

Toss a fair coin twice. Four things could happen: $HH$, $HT$, $TH$, $TT$. Since the coin is
fair, each of the four is equally likely — none is favoured over the others.

Assigning a **probability** to an experiment means giving every outcome a weight between 0
and 1, so the weights across the whole sample space sum to 1 — matching how often that
outcome would actually turn up across many repetitions (its **relative frequency**). Call
the weight of an event its `chance`:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'chance of event A', symbol: 'P(A)' },
]} />

`chance` obeys three basic rules, whatever the experiment:

$$
0 \le P(A) \le 1,
\qquad P(\varnothing) = 0,
\qquad P(S) = 1.
$$

**Why do mutually exclusive events matter here?** Because their chances add straight
across, with nothing to subtract. If $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots$ never overlap,

$$
P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3 \cup \cdots) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + P(A_3) + \cdots.
$$

**When every outcome is equally likely,** `chance` collapses to counting: if the sample
space has $N$ outcomes and $n$ of them lie in event $A$,

$$
P(A) = \frac{n}{N}.
$$

`evenRoll` from §2.2 holds $n = 3$ of the $N = 6$ outcomes in $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, so
$P(\text{evenRoll}) = \tfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §2.4, Example 2.24). Back to the two coin tosses:
> $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, each outcome weighted $\tfrac14$. The event "at least 1 head"
> is $\{HH, HT, TH\}$, so its chance is
> $P(\text{at least 1 head}) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$.

## 2.5 Additive rules

A hospital finds that 80% of its patients need medication, 60% need therapy, and 50% need
both. What fraction need medication or therapy, or both? Call the two events `needsMed` and
`needsTherapy`.

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'needsMed', symbol: 'A', note: 'P(A) = 0.8' },
  { name: 'needsTherapy', symbol: 'B', note: 'P(B) = 0.6' },
]} />

**Rule 1** answers it for any two events $A$ and $B$:

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).
$$

<Figure caption="Adding P(needsMed) and P(needsTherapy) counts the overlap twice — subtracting P(needsMed ∩ needsTherapy) undoes that double-count.">
  <Venn labelA="needsMed" labelB="needsTherapy" shade="union" title="Venn diagram of needsMed union needsTherapy" />
</Figure>

Here $P(A \cup B) = 0.8 + 0.6 - 0.5 = 0.9$ (Walpole §2.5, Example 2.29): 90% of patients
need at least one of the two. Subtracting $P(A \cap B)$ undoes the double-count of the
overlap; when the two events are mutually exclusive the overlap vanishes and
$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

**Rule 2** extends this to three events, subtracting each pairwise overlap and then
restoring the triple overlap that got subtracted three times over:

$$
P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C).
$$

> **Worked example — three-event overlap.** In a class, $P(C) = \tfrac{25}{60}$ of students
> take Calculus, $P(P) = \tfrac{30}{60}$ take Physics, $P(H) = \tfrac{20}{60}$ take
> Chemistry; $P(C \cap P) = \tfrac{10}{60}$, $P(C \cap H) = \tfrac{8}{60}$,
> $P(P \cap H) = \tfrac{5}{60}$, and $P(C \cap P \cap H) = \tfrac{3}{60}$. By Rule 2,
>
> $$
> P(C \cup P \cup H) = \frac{25 + 30 + 20 - 10 - 8 - 5 + 3}{60} = \frac{55}{60} = \frac{11}{12} \approx 0.917.
> $$

A collection $\{A_1, \dots, A_k\}$ is a **partition** of $S$ when the pieces are mutually
exclusive and their union is all of $S$; their probabilities then add to 1.

`evenRoll` and its complement partition $S$ into two pieces — every outcome is one or the
other, never both — which gives **Rule 3, the complement rule**:

$$
P(A) + P(A') = 1.
$$

<Figure caption="evenRoll and its complement split S with nothing left over and nothing shared, so their chances must add to 1.">
  <Venn labelA="evenRoll" labelB="highRoll" shade="complementA" title="Venn diagram of the complement of evenRoll" />
</Figure>

The complement rule is the fastest route to "at least one" / "none" questions: find the
easy side, subtract from 1.

## 2.6 Conditional probability, independence, and the product rule

A box holds 20 fuses, 5 of them defective. You draw one fuse and set it aside, then draw a
second. Call the first draw `firstDefective` and the second `secondDefective`.

Once `firstDefective` has happened, you are no longer drawing from the original box of 20.
You're drawing from what's left: 19 fuses, only 4 of them defective. The chance
`secondDefective` happens *given that* `firstDefective` already did is a chance computed
inside that smaller, 19-fuse population — a different number from the chance a fuse drawn
at random from the full box is defective. That "given that" chance, worked out inside the
shrunken population, is the **conditional probability**:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'chance of secondDefective, given firstDefective happened', symbol: 'P(B \\mid A) = \\dfrac{P(A \\cap B)}{P(A)}, \\quad P(A) > 0' },
]} />

**Why does this matter?** Because "given that" always means "inside this smaller
population now" — mixing that up with the original, unconditioned population is the single
most common mistake in this chapter (more on this in §2.8).

**Multiplication (product) rule.** The chance that both `firstDefective` *and*
`secondDefective` happen is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second *given*
the first already happened. This is the standard tool for a sequence of dependent steps —
drawing items one after another without replacement is the classic case:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B).
$$

> **Worked example — the fuse box.** $P(\text{firstDefective}) = \tfrac{5}{20}$. Given that,
> only 4 of the remaining 19 fuses are defective, so
> $P(\text{secondDefective} \mid \text{firstDefective}) = \tfrac{4}{19}$. Both together:
>
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = \frac{5}{20}\cdot\frac{4}{19} = \frac{1}{19}.
> $$

(This is the same identity as the conditional-probability definition above, rearranged —
but it earns its own reflex: whenever a problem reads "first this happens, then that
happens," multiply. Do not confuse it with the *counting* multiplication rule of §2.3,
which multiplies counts of ways, not probabilities.)

**Independence.** A town's fire engine is available when needed with probability 0.98, and
its ambulance with probability 0.92, and the two operate independently of each other.
"Independently" means knowing one is available tells you nothing about the other — any one
of the following equivalent statements confirms it:

$$
P(A \mid B) = P(A), \qquad P(B \mid A) = P(B), \qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B).
$$

The chance that *both* are available for a given emergency is

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) = (0.98)(0.92) = 0.9016.
$$

Independence extends to several events at once: $E_1, \dots, E_k$ are independent when
$P(E_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap E_{i_k}) = P(E_{i_1}) \cdots P(E_{i_k})$ for *every* subset of
them, not just pairwise.

> **Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent — the two are commonly confused.**
> Take a single lottery ticket: `wins` and `loses` are mutually exclusive — a ticket can't
> do both — but they are about as *dependent* as two events can get. Knowing `wins`
> happened tells you for certain `loses` did not, so $P(\text{loses} \mid \text{wins}) = 0
> \neq P(\text{loses})$ whenever $P(\text{loses}) > 0$. Independent events, by contrast,
> must have $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) > 0$ whenever $P(A), P(B) > 0$ — so two events with
> positive probability can never be both mutually exclusive and independent.

> **Worked example — conditioning shrinks the sample space.** Two fair dice are rolled.
> *Given* that the total is 7, what's the chance the first die showed a 4?

<Figure caption="6 of the 36 outcomes total 7; only one of those six — (4, 3), ringed — has a 4 on the first die.">
  <DiceGrid
    title="6 by 6 grid with the six outcomes summing to 7 highlighted, and (4,3) ringed"
    highlight={[[1, 6], [2, 5], [3, 4], [4, 3], [5, 2], [6, 1]]}
    emphasize={[[4, 3]]}
  />
</Figure>

> The total of 7 occurs in 6 equally likely ways, and only $(4,3)$ has a 4 first:
>
> $$
> P(\text{first}=4 \mid \text{sum}=7) = \frac{P(\text{first}=4 \,\cap\, \text{sum}=7)}{P(\text{sum}=7)} = \frac{1/36}{6/36} = \frac{1}{6}.
> $$

## 2.7 Bayes' rule

A factory buys parts from three machines. `machine1` makes 30% of the output, `machine2`
makes 45%, `machine3` makes 25%. Their defect rates are 2%, 3%, and 2% respectively. A part
comes off the line and turns out to be `defective`.

**Question 1 — before you know which machine made it, what's the overall chance a part is
defective?** Each machine contributes its share of output times its own defect rate:

$$
P(A) = (0.30)(0.02) + (0.45)(0.03) + (0.25)(0.02) = 0.0245.
$$

This is the **theorem of total probability**, stated for any partition $B_1, \dots, B_k$ of
$S$ — mutually exclusive events that together cover everything, each with $P(B_i) \neq 0$:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'machine1, machine2, machine3 — a partition of every part made', symbol: 'B_1, B_2, B_3' },
  { name: 'defective', symbol: 'A' },
  { name: 'overall chance a part is defective', symbol: 'P(A) = \\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\\,P(A \\mid B_i)' },
]} />

<Figure minWidth={470} caption="Multiply along each path for that path's chance; add the three defective-leaf products to get the overall 2.45% defect rate.">
  <ProbTree
    title="Probability tree: three machines branching into defective or not defective, with path products at the leaves"
    root="1 part"
    branches={[
      { label: 'machine1 (30%)', prob: '0.30', leaves: [
        { label: 'defective', prob: '0.02', product: '0.30 × 0.02 = 0.006' },
        { label: 'not defective', prob: '0.98', product: '0.30 × 0.98 = 0.294' },
      ] },
      { label: 'machine2 (45%)', prob: '0.45', leaves: [
        { label: 'defective', prob: '0.03', product: '0.45 × 0.03 = 0.0135' },
        { label: 'not defective', prob: '0.97', product: '0.45 × 0.97 = 0.4365' },
      ] },
      { label: 'machine3 (25%)', prob: '0.25', leaves: [
        { label: 'defective', prob: '0.02', product: '0.25 × 0.02 = 0.005' },
        { label: 'not defective', prob: '0.98', product: '0.25 × 0.98 = 0.245' },
      ] },
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

**Question 2 — the part turned out defective. What's the chance it came from `machine3`?**
This reverses the conditioning: Question 1 went from "which machine" to "chance of
defective"; this goes the other way, from "it's defective" back to "which machine." That
reversal is **Bayes' rule**:

<Notation rows={[
  { name: 'updated chance it was machine_r, now that the part is known to be defective', symbol: 'P(B_r \\mid A) = \\dfrac{P(B_r)\\,P(A \\mid B_r)}{\\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\\,P(A \\mid B_i)}' },
]} />

Think of the $B_i$ as competing **causes** and $A$ as the **evidence**: Bayes weighs each
cause by how probable it was on its own ($P(B_i)$) *and* by how well it explains the
evidence ($P(A \mid B_i)$).

> **Worked example** (Walpole §2.7, Examples 2.41–2.42). Using the numbers above, the
> chance a defective part came from `machine3` is
>
> $$
> P(B_3 \mid A) = \frac{(0.25)(0.02)}{0.0245} = \frac{0.005}{0.0245} \approx 0.204.
> $$

## 2.8 Potential misconceptions and hazards

Mistakes this chapter actually punishes, gathered in one place:

- **Mutually exclusive is not independent.** §2.6 covers this in full; the short version is
  worth repeating: two events with positive probability can never be both. Treating
  disjoint events as independent (or vice versa) is the most common conceptual slip in
  the chapter.
- **Equally likely is an assumption, not a default.** The formula $P(A) = n/N$ (§2.4) only
  holds when every outcome genuinely carries the same weight. A loaded die, a biased coin,
  or a sample space built from unequal groups breaks it — check the assumption before you
  start counting, don't reach for it out of habit.
- **Conditioning is not symmetric.** `secondDefective` given `firstDefective` is a
  different question from `firstDefective` given `secondDefective` — swapping which event
  you condition on silently swaps cause and evidence, so $P(A \mid B) \neq P(B \mid A)$ in
  general. Bayes' rule (§2.7) exists precisely to convert one into the other correctly; the
  error shows up when someone assumes they're already equal instead of running the
  conversion.
- **The additive rule needs the overlap subtracted.** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
  (§2.5): dropping the $-P(A \cap B)$ term double-counts the overlap. The opposite slip —
  subtracting an overlap for events that are already mutually exclusive, where it is
  already 0 — is just as common.
- **Draws without replacement are not independent.** Removing an item shrinks the
  population for every draw after it, so $P(B \mid A) \neq P(B)$ in general (§2.6). Use the
  product rule with a genuinely conditional second factor; reaching for $P(A)P(B)$ here
  quietly assumes independence the setup doesn't have.
