## Foundations

- **Sample space** $S$ — the set of *all* possible outcomes of an experiment. Write it by
  **listing** its outcomes inside braces: $S = \{H, T\}$ for one toss of a coin, and
  $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ for one roll of a die. The order you list them in carries no
  meaning, and no outcome appears twice. When an outcome has several parts, list it as an
  **ordered pair** — rolling two dice gives $S = \{(1,1), (1,2), \dots, (6,6)\}$, all $36$
  of them, where $(2,5)$ and $(5,2)$ are *different* outcomes. When listing everything is
  impractical, give the **rule** the outcomes satisfy instead,
  $S = \{x \mid x \text{ is a city with over a million people}\}$, read "the set of all $x$
  such that ...". $S$ is **discrete** when its outcomes are finite or countably infinite,
  and **continuous** when it contains an interval of real numbers.
- **Event** — any subset of $S$, written the same way: "the roll is even" is the event
  $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$. The **complement** $A'$ is everything in $S$ not in $A$, here
  $A' = \{1, 3, 5\}$; the **intersection** $A \cap B$ is the outcomes in *both*; the
  **union** $A \cup B$ is the outcomes in *either*. Events are **mutually exclusive**
  (disjoint) when $A \cap B = \varnothing$.

For a sample space of $N$ **equally likely** outcomes, $n$ of which lie in $A$,

$$
P(A) = \frac{n}{N},
\qquad 0 \le P(A) \le 1,
\qquad P(\varnothing) = 0,
\qquad P(S) = 1.
$$

Both counts are just lengths of the lists above: the even-roll event $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$ holds
$n = 3$ of the $N = 6$ outcomes in $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, so $P(A) = \tfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Additive rule** and its consequences:

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B),
\qquad
P(A) + P(A') = 1.
$$

When $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive the overlap vanishes, so $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §2.5, Example 2.29). With $P(A) = 0.8$, $P(B) = 0.6$ and
> $P(A \cap B) = 0.5$: $\;P(A \cup B) = 0.8 + 0.6 - 0.5 = 0.9$.

## Counting techniques

When outcomes are equally likely, probability reduces to counting. Pick the right tool:

- **Multiplication rule** — a sequence of $k$ operations performable in $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$
  ways can be done together in $\,n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k\,$ ways.
- **Permutations** — *ordered* arrangements. All $n$ objects: $n!$. Taking $r$ of $n$:

$$
{}_{n}P_{r} = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!},
\qquad n! = n(n-1)\cdots(2)(1), \quad 0! = 1.
$$

- **Non‑distinct objects** — arrangements of $n$ items, $n_i$ of kind $i$:
  $\;\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2! \cdots n_k!}$.
- **Combinations** — *unordered* selections of $r$ from $n$ (order does **not** matter):

$$
\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}.
$$

Here $n$ is the number of objects available and $r$ the number arranged or chosen.

> **Worked examples.**
> - A committee of $3$ is chosen from $10$ people (order irrelevant):
>   $\;\binom{10}{3} = \dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!} = 120$ ways.
> - Distinct arrangements of the letters in **STATISTICS** (S, T each ×3, I ×2, A, C ×1):
>   $\;\dfrac{10!}{3!\,3!\,2!\,1!\,1!} = 50{,}400$.

## Conditional probability and independence

The **conditional probability** of $B$ given that $A$ has occurred is

$$
P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}, \qquad P(A) > 0.
$$

Rearranging gives the **product (multiplicative) rule** $\,P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)$.
Two events are **independent** when knowing one tells you nothing about the other —
equivalently $P(B \mid A) = P(B)$ — in which case the product rule collapses to

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B).
$$

> **Worked example — dependent draw.** A box holds $20$ fuses, $5$ defective; two are drawn
> *without replacement*. The probability both are defective is
>
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = \frac{5}{20}\cdot\frac{4}{19} = \frac{1}{19}.
> $$

> **Worked example — conditioning shrinks the sample space.** Two fair dice are rolled.
> *Given* that the sum is $7$, find $P(\text{first die} = 4)$. The sum $7$ occurs in $6$
> equally likely ways $\{(1,6),\dots,(6,1)\}$, and only $(4,3)$ has a $4$ first:
>
> $$
> P(\text{first}=4 \mid \text{sum}=7) = \frac{P(\text{first}=4 \,\cap\, \text{sum}=7)}{P(\text{sum}=7)} = \frac{1/36}{6/36} = \frac{1}{6}.
> $$

## Bayes' theorem

Let $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_k$ be a **partition** of $S$ — mutually exclusive events that
together cover everything, each with $P(B_i) \neq 0$. For any event $A$, the **theorem of
total probability** assembles $P(A)$ from the pieces:

$$
P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\,P(A \mid B_i).
$$

**Bayes' rule** then reverses the conditioning — from the *likelihoods* $P(A \mid B_i)$ back
to the *posterior* $P(B_r \mid A)$:

$$
P(B_r \mid A) = \frac{P(B_r)\,P(A \mid B_r)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\,P(A \mid B_i)}.
$$

Think of the $B_i$ as competing **causes** and $A$ as the **evidence**: Bayes weighs each
cause by how probable it was ($P(B_i)$) *and* how well it explains the evidence
($P(A \mid B_i)$).

> **Worked example** (Walpole §2.7, Examples 2.41–2.42). Machines $B_1, B_2, B_3$ make
> $30\%$, $45\%$, $25\%$ of output, with defect rates $2\%$, $3\%$, $2\%$. A product is found
> defective ($A$).
>
> $$
> P(A) = (0.30)(0.02) + (0.45)(0.03) + (0.25)(0.02) = 0.0245.
> $$
>
> The chance it came from machine $B_3$ is
>
> $$
> P(B_3 \mid A) = \frac{(0.25)(0.02)}{0.0245} = \frac{0.005}{0.0245} \approx 0.204.
> $$
