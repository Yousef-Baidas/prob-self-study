## 2.1 Sample space

- **Sample space** $S$ — the set of *all* possible outcomes of an experiment. Write it by
  **listing** its outcomes inside braces: $S = \{H, T\}$ for one toss of a coin, and
  $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ for one roll of a die. The order you list them in carries no
  meaning, and no outcome appears twice. When an outcome has several parts, list it as an
  **ordered pair** — rolling two dice gives $S = \{(1,1), (1,2), \dots, (6,6)\}$, all $36$
  of them, where $(2,5)$ and $(5,2)$ are *different* outcomes: the order *within* an
  outcome carries meaning even though the order you list outcomes in does not. When
  listing everything is impractical, give the **rule** the outcomes satisfy instead,
  $S = \{x \mid x \text{ is a city with over a million people}\}$, read "the set of all $x$
  such that …". $S$ is **discrete** when its outcomes are finite or countably infinite, and
  **continuous** when it contains an interval of real numbers.
- **Tree diagram** — when an experiment runs in stages, a tree lists sample points
  systematically: branch once per stage, and each root-to-tip path spells out one sample
  point. Flip a coin, and flip again only if it lands heads — otherwise roll a die once —
  gives $S = \{HH, HT, T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6\}$, one outcome per path.

## 2.2 Events

- **Event** — any subset of $S$, written the same way: "the roll is even" is the event
  $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$. The **complement** $A'$ is everything in $S$ not in $A$, here
  $A' = \{1, 3, 5\}$; the **intersection** $A \cap B$ is the outcomes in *both*; the
  **union** $A \cup B$ is the outcomes in *either*. Events are **mutually exclusive**
  (disjoint) when $A \cap B = \varnothing$ — they share no outcomes, so at most one of
  them can occur on a given trial.
- Event combinations follow ordinary set algebra: unions and intersections commute and
  distribute over each other, and **De Morgan's laws** turn a complement of a union or
  intersection into the complement of the pieces:

$$
(A \cup B)' = A' \cap B', \qquad (A \cap B)' = A' \cup B'.
$$

  These are the go-to move whenever a question is phrased as a complement of a compound
  event — "neither $A$ nor $B$" is $(A \cup B)' = A' \cap B'$.

## 2.3 Counting sample points

When outcomes are equally likely, probability reduces to counting. Pick the right tool:

- **Multiplication rule** — a sequence of $k$ operations performable in $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$
  ways can be done together in $\,n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k\,$ ways.
- **Permutations** — *ordered* arrangements. All $n$ objects: $n!$. Taking $r$ of $n$,
  written ${}_{n}P_{r}$ or $P(n, r)$ — the two-argument $P$ is a count, not a probability:

$$
{}_{n}P_{r} = P(n, r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!},
\qquad n! = n(n-1)\cdots(2)(1), \quad 0! = 1.
$$

- **Non‑distinct objects** — arrangements of $n$ items, $n_i$ of kind $i$:
  $\;\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\,n_2! \cdots n_k!}$.
- **Combinations** — *unordered* selections of $r$ from $n$ (order does **not** matter).
  The same number is written three ways — ${}_{n}C_{r}$, $C(n, r)$ and $\binom{n}{r}$ —
  and all three are read "$n$ choose $r$":

$$
{}_{n}C_{r} = C(n, r) = \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}.
$$

Here $n$ is the number of objects available and $r$ the number arranged or chosen.

> **Worked examples.**
> - A committee of $3$ is chosen from $10$ people (order irrelevant):
>   $\;{}_{10}C_{3} = \binom{10}{3} = \dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!} = 120$ ways.
> - Distinct arrangements of the letters in **STATISTICS** (S, T each ×3, I ×2, A, C ×1):
>   $\;\dfrac{10!}{3!\,3!\,2!\,1!\,1!} = 50{,}400$.

## 2.4 Probability of an event

- Assigning a **probability** to an experiment means giving every sample point a weight
  between $0$ and $1$ so that the weights across all of $S$ sum to $1$ — matching the
  intuitive **relative frequency** of that outcome across many repetitions. The
  probability of an event $A$ is the sum of the weights of the sample points inside it,
  written $P(A)$, and it obeys the basic rules:

$$
0 \le P(A) \le 1,
\qquad P(\varnothing) = 0,
\qquad P(S) = 1.
$$

- If $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots$ is a sequence of **mutually exclusive** events, probabilities
  add straight across the whole sequence:

$$
P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3 \cup \cdots) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + P(A_3) + \cdots.
$$

- When the $N$ outcomes of $S$ are **equally likely** and $n$ of them lie in $A$,

$$
P(A) = \frac{n}{N}.
$$

  The even-roll event $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$ holds $n = 3$ of the $N = 6$ outcomes in
  $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, so $P(A) = \tfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

> **Worked example** (Walpole §2.4, Example 2.24). A coin is tossed twice:
> $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$. If the coin is fair, each outcome carries weight $\tfrac14$.
> The event "at least $1$ head" is $A = \{HH, HT, TH\}$, so
> $P(A) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$.

## 2.5 Additive rules

- **Rule 1** — for any two events $A$ and $B$:

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).
$$

  Subtracting $P(A \cap B)$ undoes the double-count of the overlap; when $A$ and $B$ are
  mutually exclusive the overlap vanishes and $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.
- **Rule 2** extends this to three events, subtracting each pairwise overlap and then
  restoring the triple overlap that got subtracted three times over:

$$
P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C).
$$

- A collection $\{A_1, \dots, A_k\}$ is a **partition** of $S$ when the $A_i$ are mutually
  exclusive and their union is all of $S$; their probabilities then add to $1$.
- **Rule 3 (complement rule)** — $A$ and $A'$ partition $S$ into two pieces, so

$$
P(A) + P(A') = 1.
$$

  It is the fastest route to "at least one" / "none" questions: find the easy side,
  subtract from $1$.

> **Worked examples.**
> - (Walpole §2.5, Example 2.29.) With $P(A) = 0.8$, $P(B) = 0.6$ and
>   $P(A \cap B) = 0.5$: $\;P(A \cup B) = 0.8 + 0.6 - 0.5 = 0.9$.
> - **Three-event overlap.** In a class, $P(C) = \tfrac{25}{60}$ of students take
>   Calculus, $P(P) = \tfrac{30}{60}$ take Physics, $P(H) = \tfrac{20}{60}$ take
>   Chemistry; $P(C \cap P) = \tfrac{10}{60}$, $P(C \cap H) = \tfrac{8}{60}$,
>   $P(P \cap H) = \tfrac{5}{60}$, and $P(C \cap P \cap H) = \tfrac{3}{60}$. By Rule 2,
>   $$
>   P(C \cup P \cup H) = \frac{25 + 30 + 20 - 10 - 8 - 5 + 3}{60} = \frac{55}{60} = \frac{11}{12} \approx 0.917.
>   $$

## 2.6 Conditional probability, independence, and the product rule

The **conditional probability** of $B$ given that $A$ has occurred is

$$
P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}, \qquad P(A) > 0.
$$

**Multiplication (product) rule.** The probability that both $A$ *and* $B$ occur is the
probability of the first, times the probability of the second *given* the first has
occurred — the standard tool for a sequence of dependent steps, such as drawing items
one after another without replacement:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B).
$$

(This is the same identity as the conditional-probability definition above, rearranged —
but it is worth keeping as its own reflex: whenever a problem reads "first this happens,
then that happens," multiply. Do not confuse it with the *counting* multiplication rule
of §2.3, which multiplies counts of ways rather than probabilities.)

**Independence.** Two events are **independent** when knowing one occurred tells you
nothing about the other; any one of the following equivalent statements confirms it:

$$
P(A \mid B) = P(A), \qquad P(B \mid A) = P(B), \qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B).
$$

Independence extends to several events at once: $E_1, \dots, E_k$ are independent when
$P(E_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap E_{i_k}) = P(E_{i_1}) \cdots P(E_{i_k})$ for *every* subset of
them, not just pairwise.

> **Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent — the two are commonly confused.**
> Mutually exclusive events are the most *dependent* pair possible: if
> $A \cap B = \varnothing$, then knowing $A$ occurred tells you for certain $B$ did not,
> so $P(B \mid A) = 0 \neq P(B)$ whenever $P(B) > 0$. Independent events, by contrast,
> must have $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) > 0$ whenever $P(A), P(B) > 0$ — so two events with
> positive probability can never be both mutually exclusive and independent.

> **Worked example — dependent draw.** A box holds $20$ fuses, $5$ defective; two are drawn
> *without replacement*. The probability both are defective is
>
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = \frac{5}{20}\cdot\frac{4}{19} = \frac{1}{19}.
> $$

> **Worked example — conditioning shrinks the sample space.** Two fair dice are rolled.
> *Given* that the sum is $7$, find $P(\text{first die} = 4)$. The sum $7$ occurs in $6$
> equally likely ways $\{(1,6),\dots,(6,1)\}$, and only $(4,3)$ has a $4$ first:
>
> $$
> P(\text{first}=4 \mid \text{sum}=7) = \frac{P(\text{first}=4 \,\cap\, \text{sum}=7)}{P(\text{sum}=7)} = \frac{1/36}{6/36} = \frac{1}{6}.
> $$

> **Worked example — independence.** A town's fire engine is available when needed with
> probability $0.98$, and its ambulance with probability $0.92$, the two operating
> independently. The probability that *both* are available for a given emergency is
>
> $$
> P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) = (0.98)(0.92) = 0.9016.
> $$

## 2.7 Bayes' rule

Let $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_k$ be a **partition** of $S$ — mutually exclusive events that
together cover everything, each with $P(B_i) \neq 0$. For any event $A$, the **theorem of
total probability** assembles $P(A)$ from the pieces:

$$
P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\,P(A \mid B_i).
$$

**Bayes' rule** then reverses the conditioning — from the *likelihoods* $P(A \mid B_i)$ back
to the *posterior* $P(B_r \mid A)$:

$$
P(B_r \mid A) = \frac{P(B_r)\,P(A \mid B_r)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(B_i)\,P(A \mid B_i)}.
$$

Think of the $B_i$ as competing **causes** and $A$ as the **evidence**: Bayes weighs each
cause by how probable it was ($P(B_i)$) *and* how well it explains the evidence
($P(A \mid B_i)$).

> **Worked example** (Walpole §2.7, Examples 2.41–2.42). Machines $B_1, B_2, B_3$ make
> $30\%$, $45\%$, $25\%$ of output, with defect rates $2\%$, $3\%$, $2\%$. A product is found
> defective ($A$).
>
> $$
> P(A) = (0.30)(0.02) + (0.45)(0.03) + (0.25)(0.02) = 0.0245.
> $$
>
> The chance it came from machine $B_3$ is
>
> $$
> P(B_3 \mid A) = \frac{(0.25)(0.02)}{0.0245} = \frac{0.005}{0.0245} \approx 0.204.
> $$

## 2.8 Potential misconceptions and hazards

Mistakes this chapter actually punishes, gathered in one place:

- **Mutually exclusive is not independent.** §2.6 covers this in full; the short version is
  worth repeating: two events with positive probability can never be both. Treating
  disjoint events as independent (or vice versa) is the most common conceptual slip in
  the chapter.
- **Equally likely is an assumption, not a default.** $P(A) = n/N$ (§2.4) only holds when
  every sample point genuinely carries the same weight. A loaded die, a biased coin, or a
  sample space built from unequal groups breaks it — check the assumption before you
  start counting, don't reach for it out of habit.
- **$P(A \mid B) \neq P(B \mid A)$.** Swapping the conditioning event silently swaps cause
  and evidence. Bayes' rule (§2.7) exists precisely to convert one into the other
  correctly — the error shows up when someone assumes they're already equal instead of
  running the conversion.
- **The additive rule needs the overlap subtracted.** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
  (§2.5): dropping the $-P(A \cap B)$ term double-counts the overlap. The opposite slip —
  subtracting an overlap for events that are already mutually exclusive, where it is
  already $0$ — is just as common.
- **Draws without replacement are not independent.** Removing an item shrinks the sample
  space for every draw after it, so $P(B \mid A) \neq P(B)$ in general (§2.6). Use the
  product rule with a genuinely conditional second factor; reaching for $P(A)P(B)$ here
  quietly assumes independence the setup doesn't have.
